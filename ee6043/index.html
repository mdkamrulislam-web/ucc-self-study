Chips, Moore and Dennard

The one ideaVLSI means putting millions or billions of gates on one chip. For thirty years every process node made transistors smaller, faster and cheaper while the power per square millimetre stayed the same (Dennard scaling). Around 2005 the supply voltage stopped falling, power density started to climb, and progress now comes from architecture instead of clock speed.

What VLSI means

Very Large Scale Integration is the process of creating integrated circuits with millions or billions of gates. The same design techniques apply to much of FPGA design, which is why a course taught by Xilinx engineers covers both: Xilinx uses ASIC practice to design its FPGAs, and then also uses those FPGAs in applications (slide 21).

NameGates on one chip
SSI · small-scale integration1+
MSI · medium-scale integration100+
LSI · large-scale integration1,000+
VLSI · very large-scale integration10,000+, and billions today

What makes a billion-gate design possible

No engineer places a billion transistors by hand. Slide 22 lists the enablers:

  • EDA tools (electronic design automation), “the driving force behind all modern design”: simulators, synthesis, place and route, timing and power analysis.
  • HDLs such as Verilog, SystemVerilog and VHDL, so one engineer can describe and test a million-gate design.
  • Standard cells, the lowest level a digital designer works at: pre-built gates that tools can pick and place automatically (Lesson 3).
  • IP (intellectual property blocks): reusing a proven processor, memory controller or interface instead of designing it again.
  • Abstraction and scripting: work at the highest level that still answers your question, and automate everything that repeats.
Learn these · Winter 2022 Q1(a)

“Define the terms VLSI, FPGA, EDA tools, standard cell, HDL” (5 marks). One or two sentences each is enough:

  • VLSI: very large scale integration, building integrated circuits with millions to billions of transistors on one chip, and the design discipline that makes it possible.
  • FPGA: field-programmable gate array, a chip of pre-built but configurable logic (look-up tables, flip-flops, block RAM, DSP blocks, I/O) joined by programmable routing. A bitstream loaded after manufacture (“in the field”) decides what it does, and it can be reprogrammed.
  • EDA tools: electronic design automation, the software used to design, verify and implement chips, such as simulators, synthesis, place and route, and timing and power analysis (Vivado for Xilinx FPGAs).
  • Standard cell: a pre-designed, pre-characterised gate or flip-flop from a foundry library, with a fixed height so cells tile in rows, and views for layout, timing, power and simulation. Synthesis maps RTL onto these cells.
  • HDL: hardware description language (Verilog, SystemVerilog, VHDL), a language that describes hardware, including its parallelism and timing, for simulation and synthesis.

Moore’s law, as Moore actually wrote it

Gordon Moore, co-founder of Intel, wrote “Cramming more components onto integrated circuits” in 1965. It is often misquoted as “performance doubles every 18 months”, “clock speed doubles every 18 months” or “the number of transistors doubles every 18 months”. What he actually wrote (slide 26) is:

“The complexity for minimum component costs has increased at a rate of roughly a factor of two per year.”

So Moore’s law is an economic statement: at the cost-optimal point, the number of transistors on a chip doubles every year (revised in 1975 to every two years). The lecture separates two aspects:

  • Economic aspect: at constant cost, transistor count doubles every year, later every 1.5–2 years. This is no longer true; cost per transistor has flattened (Lesson 2).
  • Scaling aspect: transistors still shrink, but more slowly and with more difficulty at each generation. The largest FPGA in 2019 held 35 billion transistors (slide 15).

Dennard scaling: why shrinking used to be free

In 1974 Robert Dennard showed that as transistors shrink, their power density stays constant. The dynamic power of a chip is

Dynamic power P∝NCFV2

N = number of transistors, C = capacitance being switched, F = clock frequency, V = supply voltage. This same equation comes back in every power question (Winter 2022 Q1c, 2024 Q4, 2025 Q4).

Slide 30 lists what one shrink does to each term:

Effect of scalingPower
1. Area reduces, so capacitance reducesreduces
2. Voltage reducesreduces
3. Area reduces, so more transistors fitincreases
4. Smaller capacitance allows a higher Fmaxincreases

For constant power, effects 1 and 2 cancel 3 and 4. With numbers: shrink every dimension and the voltage by a factor 0.7. Capacitance falls to 0.7, frequency can rise to 1/0.7 ≈ 1.4, so the power of one transistor falls to 0.7 × 0.7² × 1.4 ≈ 0.49. But twice as many transistors now fit in the same area (1/0.7² ≈ 2), so the power per square millimetre is 2 × 0.49 ≈ 1: unchanged. You got twice the transistors, 40% more speed and the same heat, every generation.

The end of the free lunch

From the 1970s until about 2005, each shrink made chips smaller, faster, cheaper, more efficient and more capable at once. Then (slide 31):

  • Voltage scaling slowed down, so the V² benefit shrank (slide 31: “V² benefit reduced”). The threshold voltage cannot keep falling without leakage current rising exponentially.
  • Leakage was not accounted for in Dennard’s analysis, and becomes significant at small nodes: transistors draw current even when they are not switching (static power).
  • Power density now increases with each node, and a chip can only shed so much heat.

The result is the power wall: Fmax is limited by heat, not by the transistors, and some of the chip must stay switched off at any moment (dark silicon). The trend chart on slide 32 shows it: transistor counts keep climbing after 2005, while clock frequency, single-thread performance and typical power flatten, and the number of cores starts to rise instead.

Exam angle

No past paper asks you to compute scaling factors. What is examined is the vocabulary (VLSI, EDA, HDL, standard cell, FPGA) and the power equation: the two kinds of power are dynamic (switching: N·C·F·V², where F is the clock frequency and N should count only the nodes that actually toggle; many texts write this with an activity factor α, the fraction of nodes switching each cycle) and static (leakage), and every reduction technique attacks one of those terms.

Check yourself
What did Moore actually predict in 1965?

That the number of components per chip at the minimum-cost point would double every year (revised to every two years in 1975). It is about economics, not clock speed or performance.

Under Dennard scaling, what happens to power density when a chip moves to the next node?

It stays the same. Each transistor uses about half the power (0.7 × 0.49 × 1.4 ≈ 0.49), and twice as many fit in each square millimetre.

Why did clock frequencies stop rising around 2005?

The supply voltage stopped falling (the threshold voltage can’t drop further without huge leakage), so shrinking no longer cut the power per transistor enough. With twice the transistors per mm² each node, power density rose until cooling limited the clock: the power wall.

Nodes, cost and yield

The one ideaEach new process node halves the area of a circuit, but designing and making masks for it costs more every time, and random defects kill big dies disproportionately. So the node a company chooses is an economic decision as much as a technical one.

What a “node” is

Each generation of scaling is a process node, named “X nanometres”, for example 16 nm. The name once was the transistor’s gate length, so a 180 nm node had 180 nm gates. That no longer holds: node names now track density, with some marketing involved (slide 33).

  • Each node shrinks X and Y by about 0.7, so area shrinks by 0.7² ≈ 0.5: the same circuit takes half the silicon.
  • Example nodes: 90, 65, 40, 28, 20, 16 nm, each roughly 0.7 times the one before.
  • ASML proposed a “standard node” computed from two real dimensions: 0.14 × (CPHP × MMHP)0.67, where CPHP is the contacted poly half-pitch (half the contacted gate pitch, the centre-to-centre distance between neighbouring gates) and MMHP the minimum metal half-pitch.

Slide 33 says “180um Node => 180um Gate Length”. It means 180 nm (0.18 µm).

One subtlety from slide 37: the same chip can still be cheaper on a newer node when a smaller die improves yield. That is the next idea.

Defect density and yield

A wafer carries random defects, measured as defect density D (defects per cm²). A die that catches one is usually scrap. The fraction of good dies is the yield. The simplest model assumes defects land independently (a Poisson process):

Yield models YPoisson=e−ADYMurphy=(1−e−ADAD)2

A = die area in cm² (100 mm² = 1 cm²). Murphy’s model allows the defect density to vary from wafer to wafer, which is kinder to large dies. The yield calculator quoted on slide 38 uses Murphy’s model: it reproduces the slide’s 61.9% for the 100 mm² die and 40.2% for the 200 mm² die, both at D = 0.5/cm² (the slide’s 40.16% corresponds to a square die of about 14.1 mm, 199 mm²). Poisson would give 61% and 37%.

Doubling the area took the yield from 61% to 40%. With half as many candidate dies per wafer and a lower fraction of them good, the slide’s wafers end up with 357 good small dies and only 118 good large ones: three times the cost per good die for twice the area. This is why very large chips are expensive, and why the industry is moving to chiplets.

What comes next

Slides 39–40 close the story. With dark silicon, extra transistors are best spent on specialised circuits that switch on only when needed. Nodes continue (5 nm ramping, 3 nm arriving), transistor shapes change from planar to FinFET to gate-all-around to stacked, and architecture becomes the main lever:

  • Domain-specific architectures, such as Google’s TPU for machine learning.
  • 3D stacking and chiplets: several smaller dies in one package, each with a high yield.
  • 2.5D stacking (Xilinx’s stacked silicon interconnect, SSIT): FPGA slices placed side by side on a silicon interposer, joined by microbumps and through-silicon vias. Xilinx pioneered it.
Exam angle

None of the three past papers asks for yield or node calculations, so treat this lesson as background for the definitions and for the project’s area report. The intuition to keep: cost per good die grows faster than die area.

Check yourself
Why does doubling the die area more than halve the good dies per wafer?

Two effects multiply. Half as many dies fit on the wafer, and each large die is more likely to catch a defect, so the yield also drops (61% to 40% on slide 38).

What does “fabless” mean, and who pioneered it?

A company that designs chips but owns no fab and pays a foundry to make them. Xilinx pioneered the model in 1985.

Give two reasons a company might stay on 28 nm instead of moving to 16 nm.

Design and tape-out costs are much higher at 16 nm, and cost per transistor has flattened, so without very high volume the move doesn’t pay.

Standard cells and the design flow

The one ideaA digital chip is assembled from a library of pre-characterised gates called standard cells. You write RTL; synthesis turns it into a netlist of cells; place and route arranges and wires them; static timing analysis checks every path at each step. An FPGA flow has the same steps, except the “cells” are the FPGA’s fixed look-up tables and flip-flops.

Analogue and digital design

Analogue IC designDigital IC design
WhyThe world is analogue: audio, sensors, radio signalsThe trend is to go digital as early as possible (the RFSoC samples radio signals directly), and digital is the core of every system
ExamplesADCs, DACs, PLLsProcessors, DSP, networking, phones, TVs, ADAS, GPUs
DesignMostly simulation-based, with little automationHeavily automated
ChallengesInflexible, costly, sensitive to manufacturing variation, manual layoutMillion-gate designs, complex interactions, performance and power walls, verification

Engineers usually specialise in one, but a working knowledge of both is valuable (slide 42). Some traditionally analogue blocks are now built digitally, such as the all-digital PLL.

Standard cells

All digital IC design uses standard cells: the transistors of an inverter, a NAND gate or a flip-flop are drawn once by the library team and then treated as a gate (slide 43). Synthesis maps your HDL onto cells picked from the library, and place and route lays them out automatically to meet the constraints you give it.

Each cell comes with several views, each modelling the transistors at the accuracy a tool needs without wasting computing time: physical (layout, and the LEF abstract used for placement), timing and power (the .lib file), noise, leakage, behavioural (a simulation model) and voltage domain.

  • Pre-characterised for one process: the transistor detail is hidden behind measured delay and power tables.
  • Fixed height, variable width, so cells abut in rows. Double-height cells are sometimes allowed.
  • Power rails top and bottom; some metal layers are used inside the cell (for example M1 for power, M1 and M2 to connect the transistors), leaving the upper layers for routing between cells.

Check yourself
Why are standard cells all the same height?

So they abut in rows and share the power rails at the top and bottom. The router can then treat every row the same way; only the width changes from cell to cell.

What is the FPGA equivalent of place and route?

Implementation in Vivado: it places the synthesised LUTs, flip-flops and other primitives into fixed sites on the device and routes the programmable interconnect between them.

Why not use the fastest (LVT or uLVT) cells everywhere?

A lower threshold voltage leaks much more current. Designers use fast cells only on the paths that need them and standard cells elsewhere to keep static power down.

Modules, ports and parameters

The one ideaVerilog describes hardware, where everything runs at once. The unit of description is the module: a box with ports. You build bigger boxes by placing copies (instances) of smaller ones inside, which creates a hierarchy, and parameters let one module serve many widths and sizes.

What Verilog is for

Verilog is a hardware description language. Hardware is parallel: every gate works all the time, which a sequential programming language cannot model (slide 4). The same language is used three ways:

  • Behavioural models, for exploring a design idea and for verification (testbenches).
  • RTL (register transfer level) models, which describe the design down to the level of individual registers and what happens between them on each clock cycle, so they are cycle-accurate. RTL is what synthesis turns into gates.
  • Testbenches, which drive stimulus into a design and capture what comes out (Lesson 13).

Only part of the language can become hardware. Delays such as #5 and $display are for simulation only; synthesis ignores them. ASIC synthesis ignores initial blocks too; FPGA tools such as Vivado use them only to set the power-up values of registers and the starting contents of memories (for example with $readmemh), never to describe behaviour over time. Lesson 12 lists the lecturer’s coding rules for the part that synthesises.

The module

A module starts with module and its name and ends with endmodule. Everything in between is the body (slide 8). A module can be extended with a port list: ports are how it talks to its environment, like the pins of a chip (slide 9). There are two styles for declaring them (slide 10):

Verilog-1995: list, then directions

module shift_register (Clk, ShiftIn, ParallelIn,
                       load, ShiftEn, ShiftOut, RegContent);
  input        Clk;
  input        ShiftIn;
  input  [3:0] ParallelIn;
  input        load;
  input        ShiftEn;
  output       ShiftOut;
  output [3:0] RegContent;
  // body of the block
endmodule

Verilog-2001 ANSI style: together

module shift_register (
  input        Clk,
  input        ShiftIn,
  input  [3:0] ParallelIn,
  input        load,
  input        ShiftEn,
  output       ShiftOut,
  output [3:0] RegContent
);
  // body of the block
endmodule

Both describe the same box. The ANSI style keeps the name, direction and width of each port on one line, so there is one list to maintain instead of two. The width [3:0] means four bits numbered 3 (most significant) down to 0.

Instances and hierarchy

You instantiate a module when you use it inside another, higher-level module. Each copy gets an instance name, and the nesting of instances is the design hierarchy (slide 7). Every instance, and every signal inside it, then has a hierarchical name: top.inst1.inst2 is an instance and top.inst1.inst2.q a signal inside it.

An instance’s ports can be connected in two ways (slide 11). Here the testbench from the slide connects the shift register by name, which is the recommended method:

module tb;
  reg        Clk;
  reg        din;
  reg  [3:0] ParallelIn;
  reg        load;
  reg        ShiftEn;
  wire       dout;
  wire [3:0] RegContent;

  shift_register dut (
    .Clk        (Clk),         // .port_name (signal_in_this_module)
    .ShiftIn    (din),         // names need not match
    .ParallelIn (ParallelIn),
    .load       (load),
    .ShiftEn    (ShiftEn),
    .ShiftOut   (dout),
    .RegContent (RegContent)
  );
endmodule

Connecting by order would be shift_register dut (Clk, din, ParallelIn, load, ShiftEn, dout, RegContent);. It works only while the order matches the declaration exactly. Swap two one-bit signals and it still compiles, silently wired wrong, which is why the slide says “avoid”: it raises the risk of bugs and makes debugging harder.

Parameters

A parameter is a constant declared inside a module that sets some of its characteristics, typically a width or a depth (slides 12–13). It can be overridden for each instance, so one description can produce an 8-bit copy here and a 32-bit copy there. The lecturer’s shift register (this is also Lab 3, and Winter 2022 Q4):

// Lecture 2, slide 36 (Example 2): 4-bit parallel-in, left-shift register
module shift_register #(parameter D_W = 4) (
  input              Clk,
  input              ShiftIn,
  input  [D_W-1:0]   ParallelIn,
  input              load,
  input              ShiftEn,
  output             ShiftOut,
  output [D_W-1:0]   RegContent
);
  localparam MSB_INDEX        = D_W-1;
  localparam MSB_MINUS1_INDEX = D_W-2;

  reg [D_W-1:0] shift_ff;

  always @(posedge Clk) begin
    if (load)
      shift_ff <= ParallelIn;
    else if (ShiftEn)
      shift_ff <= {shift_ff[MSB_MINUS1_INDEX:0], ShiftIn};
  end

  assign ShiftOut   = shift_ff[MSB_INDEX];
  assign RegContent = shift_ff;
endmodule
  • #(parameter D_W = 4) declares the parameter with a default of 4. Every width in the module is written in terms of D_W.
  • localparam declares constants derived inside the module that must not be overridden from outside. MSB_INDEX is always D_W-1; letting a user set it separately could only produce an inconsistent module.
  • To override at instantiation: shift_register #(.D_W(8)) sr8 ( ... ); gives an 8-bit register with RegContent[7:0].
  • Parameters have local scope, which the slide prefers to a `define macro: a macro is global text substitution, visible in every file compiled after it, so two blocks can clash over the same name (“namespace pollution”).
  • The slide also mentions defparam, which changes a parameter from elsewhere in the hierarchy. It is legacy, and most coding guidelines forbid it; override at instantiation instead.

The slide’s last point is the important one: a parameter only makes a block truly configurable if it is used consistently. If one width in the module were written as [3:0] instead of [D_W-1:0], the 8-bit copy would silently break.

Slide wording

Slide 13 says D_W “define[s] the level shifter depth”. It means the shift register’s depth (its width in bits).

SystemVerilog adds .name and .* port connections and the logic type. They are fine in the project, but the lectures and past papers use Verilog-2001, so connect by name in full in exam answers.

Why is connecting ports by order risky?

The connection depends only on position. If the order differs from the module’s declaration, signals of the same width swap places without any error, and the bug shows up only in simulation, or on the board.

Why is MSB_INDEX a localparam and not a parameter?

It is derived from D_W. Making it overridable would allow an instance with D_W = 8 but MSB_INDEX = 3, an inconsistent module.

Nets, variables and values

The one ideaA net (wire) is a connection: it always shows whatever is driving it. A variable (reg) holds the last value a procedural block wrote into it. Despite the name, reg does not mean register: whether you get a gate or a flip-flop depends on how the block that writes it is written. Every bit is 0, 1, x or z.

The two you will use almost exclusively are wire and reg, and both are synthesisable (slide 16). Logic synthesis is the process that turns this description, typically at RTL, into an implementation made of logic gates.

A reg is not always a register

The name is historical. What a reg becomes depends on the block that writes it:

Becomes an AND gate

reg c;
always @(*) begin
  c = a & b;
end

Becomes a flip-flop

reg c;
always @(posedge clk) begin
  c <= a & b;
end

Both declare c as a reg because both assign it inside an always block. The first block runs whenever a or b changes, so c simply follows them: a gate. The second runs only at a rising clock edge, so c must hold its value in between: a flip-flop. Lesson 8 returns to this pair.

Declare everything

Ports default to wire (slide 17). Nets are written with continuous assignments such as assign ShiftOut = shift_ff[MSB_INDEX];.

Verilog has a trap: if an undeclared name appears in a port connection (or on the left of an assign), it quietly creates a one-bit wire with that name. Mistype .RegContent(RegContnet) and the port is connected to a new one-bit net instead of your 4-bit bus, with only a warning. (Elsewhere, such as inside an always block, an undeclared name is a compile error.) Always declare signals explicitly. A common habit in industry is to put `default_nettype none at the top of each file, which turns an undeclared name into a compile error.

Gates treat x sensibly: 0 & x = 0 (a zero forces an AND low whatever the other input is), but 1 & x = x. Likewise 1 | x = 1 and 0 | x = x. A z on a gate input behaves like x.

Real hardware has no x. On the FPGA, every flip-flop powers up to a known value set in the bitstream (0 unless you ask otherwise). An x in simulation is the simulator telling you the design does not guarantee that value, which usually means a missing reset.

Literals

Numbers are written <width>'<base><value>, with base b (binary), o (octal), d (decimal) or h (hexadecimal). From slide 19, these three are the same four bits:

initial begin
  a = 4'b1111;
  b = 4'hF;
  c = 4'd15;
end

A few rules that are not on the slide but matter in practice:

  • Underscores are allowed for readability: 8'b1010_0011.
  • A number without a width, such as 15, is 32 bits wide.
  • A value too big for its width is truncated, keeping the low bits: 4'd20 is 4'b0100. Too small and it is padded with zeros: 8'hF is 8'b0000_1111.
  • Plain decimal numbers (15, -3) are signed; based numbers (4'b1111) are unsigned unless you add s: 4'sb1111 is −1. Lesson 11 explains why this matters.

Combinational logic and the latch trap

The one ideaCombinational logic has no memory: its outputs depend only on its present inputs. Write it with assign, or with always @(*) and blocking =, and give every output a value on every path through the block. Miss one path and the hardware has to remember the old value, so synthesis builds a latch you never wanted.

Three ways to write an AND gate

Combinational logic can be inferred with a continuous assignment or a procedural (always) block, and either can describe any Boolean function (slides 20–21). All three of these are a two-input AND gate. Which one is not recommended?

Continuous assignment

wire c;
assign c = a & b;

Procedural, full list

reg c;
always @(*) begin
  c = a & b;
end

Procedural, explicit list (avoid)

reg c;
always @(a or b) begin
  c = a & b;
end

The one with the hand-written event list. It works here, but the list is easy to get wrong as the block grows. Leave out b and the simulator only re-evaluates c when a changes, so in simulation c ignores changes on b. Synthesis ignores event lists and builds a real AND gate, so the simulation and the hardware disagree. @(*) always builds the complete list (slide 22).

Rules for combinational always blocks · slide 23
  • Use a complete event list: write always @(*).
  • Use blocking assignment = (Lesson 8 explains why).
  • Assign every variable under every condition. The easy way: give each one a default value at the top of the block.

Break the last rule and an unwanted latch is inferred. If you really need storage, use a flip-flop.

How a latch sneaks in

Slide 24’s example is an up-counter’s next-state logic:

reg [3:0] count_ff, count_nxt;
always @(*) begin
  if (up) count_nxt = count_ff + 4'b1;
end

What happens when up is 0? Nothing assigns count_nxt, so it must keep the value it had. Something that keeps a value is storage, and storage that is controlled by a level (here up) instead of a clock edge is a latch: transparent while up is 1, holding while it is 0.

Latches are almost always a coding mistake in synchronous design. While a latch is open, any glitch on its input passes straight through; static timing analysis of latches is harder; and on an FPGA the tool has to configure a storage element as a latch, which Vivado reports with a warning. When you see that warning in a synthesis log, look for a missing default.

Two ways to avoid latches · slide 25

A default case item

always @(*) begin
  case ({down, up})
    2'b01:   count_nxt = count_ff + 4'b1;
    2'b10:   count_nxt = count_ff - 4'b1;
    default: count_nxt = count_ff;
  endcase
end

A default assignment first

always @(*) begin
  count_nxt = count_ff;          // default
  if (~down &  up) count_nxt = count_ff + 4'b1;
  if ( down & ~up) count_nxt = count_ff - 4'b1;
end

Both describe a 4-to-1 multiplexer selected by {down, up}: 01 counts up, 10 counts down, and 00 or 11 holds. “Holds” is fine here because it means pass count_ff through; the actual storage is the flip-flop that count_ff comes from, written in a separate clocked block.

Slide label

The multiplexer on slide 25 labels its inputs count_ff-4 and count_ff+4, but the code adds and subtracts 4'b1, a 4-bit one. The counter steps by one.

The only difference is the event list. Blocking and non-blocking

    
The one ideaA blocking assignment = updates the variable immediately, like a line of software. A non-blocking assignment <= reads its right-hand side now but writes the result only after every block triggered at that moment has run, so all right-hand sides are read before any left-hand side changes. That is exactly how flip-flops behave, so use <= for flip-flops and = for combinational logic.

  


  
Two circuits, both written with reg

  

    
An AND gate · slide 37


// procedural assignment
reg c;
always @(*) begin
  c = a & b;
end

    

    
A registered AND gate


reg c;
always @(posedge clk) begin
  c <= a & b;
end

    

  

  
The flip-flop comes from the clocked event list, not from the <=. The choice of token decides whether the simulation behaves like the hardware when several registers update on the same edge. That is where the two differ.


  
How the simulator runs one moment

  
Simplified, a Verilog simulator handles each instant in two passes:

  
    
    
  1. Run every block that was triggered (for example by posedge clk), in an order the language does not define. A blocking = writes its variable straight away, so later statements, and blocks that happen to run later, see the new value. A non-blocking <= evaluates its right-hand side and puts the result on a list.
    2. 
    
  2. Apply the list: every scheduled <= update happens at once.
    3. 
  

  
This is the precise meaning of slide 38: blocking assignments “are executed in the order they are coded” and block the next statement until done; non-blocking assignments behave as if “executed in parallel”, so a group of them gives a predictable result.


  
The race

  
Slide 39 makes the point with two always blocks triggered by the same clock edge. The cleanest version is a swap:

  

    
Blocking: result depends on luck


always @(posedge clk) a = b;
always @(posedge clk) b = a;

    
If the simulator runs the first block first, a takes b’s value and then b copies the new a: both end up equal to the old b. Run in the other order, both end up equal to the old a. Nothing in the language says which, so the result is unpredictable.

    

    
Non-blocking: always a swap


always @(posedge clk) a <= b;
always @(posedge clk) b <= a;

    
Both right-hand sides are read first, with the old values, then both updates land together. a and b swap on every edge, whatever the order, which is what two cross-connected flip-flops do.

The same effect inside one block:

always @(posedge clk) begin
  q1 = d;
  q2 = q1;   // already the new q1, i.e. d
  q3 = q2;   // also d
end

    

    
Non-blocking: a three-stage shift register


always @(posedge clk) begin
  q1 <= d;
  q2 <= q1;  // old q1
  q3 <= q2;  // old q2
end

    

  

  
Simulated with d = 1 on the first edge and 0 after, the blocking version gives q1 q2 q3 = 111, then 000: every stage copies d on the same edge. The non-blocking version gives 100, 010, 001: a real shift register. // Lecture 3, slide 8: low-level (gate) description
module counter_gate (
  input        clk,     // counter clock
  input        reset,   // async active-high reset used to clear the counter
  output [2:0] count    // count state
);
  reg [2:0] count_ff;   // flip-flops holding the counter state
  always @(posedge clk or posedge reset) begin
    if (reset) begin
      count_ff <= 3'd0;
    end else begin
      count_ff[0] <= ~count_ff[0];
      count_ff[1] <= count_ff[0] ^ count_ff[1];
      count_ff[2] <= (count_ff[0] & count_ff[1]) ^ count_ff[2];
    end
  end
  assign count = count_ff;
endmodule

// Lecture 3, slide 9: higher-level description
module counter (
  input        clk,
  input        reset,
  output [2:0] count
);

  always @(posedge clk or posedge reset) begin

    end
  end

endmodule

FPGA resources, and one-hot

  
Winter 2024 Q3 asks what FPGA resources this counter needs. Three flip-flops (Xilinx FDCE: a D flip-flop with clock enable and asynchronous clear, because the reset is asynchronous), and next-state logic of at most three inputs per bit: D0 needs a 1-input function, D1 a 2-input one and D2 a 3-input one, so three look-up tables at most. The tools may pack two small functions into one 6-input LUT or use the dedicated carry chain for the +1. Add a global clock buffer (BUFG), input buffers for clk and reset and three output buffers.

  
Part (c) asks what changes with one-hot encoding: one flip-flop per state, exactly one of them 1 at a time. The next-state logic vanishes. Each flop copies its neighbour, so the counter becomes a ring of eight flip-flops. Reset must put the single 1 in the state-0 flop (a flop with an asynchronous preset) and 0 in the other seven. If a binary count is still needed, three OR gates encode it:


// Winter 2024 Q3(c): the same 8-state counter with one-hot encoding
module counter_onehot (
  input        clk,
  input        reset,          // async active-high
  output [2:0] count
);
  reg [7:0] state_ff;          // exactly one bit is 1: bit i = "count is i"
  always @(posedge clk or posedge reset) begin
    if (reset) state_ff <= 8'b0000_0001;                   // state 0
    else       state_ff <= {state_ff[6:0], state_ff[7]};   // rotate left: i -> i+1
  end
  // encode back to binary only if a binary count output is needed
  assign count[0] = state_ff[1] | state_ff[3] | state_ff[5] | state_ff[7];
  assign count[1] = state_ff[2] | state_ff[3] | state_ff[6] | state_ff[7];
  assign count[2] = state_ff[4] | state_ff[5] | state_ff[6] | state_ff[7];
endmodule

  
Synthesised for a 7-series FPGA (with Yosys), this module gives seven flip-flops with clear, one with preset, and three 4-input LUTs for the encoder. One-hot trades flip-flops, which FPGAs have in abundance (106,400 on the PYNQ-Z2), for simpler and faster logic.

Check yourself
    
Why is D1 = Q1 ⊕ Q0?
Q1 must toggle exactly when Q0 is 1 (a carry comes in) and hold when Q0 is 0. XOR with Q0 does precisely that: Q1 ⊕ 1 = Q1, Q1 ⊕ 0 = Q1.

    
What would D3 be for a 4-bit counter?
D3 = Q3 ⊕ (Q2·Q1·Q0): bit 3 toggles when all three bits below it are 1.

    
How many flip-flops does a one-hot 8-state counter need, and why can that still be the better choice on an FPGA?
Eight. The next-state logic disappears (each flop copies its neighbour), so paths are short and fast, and FPGAs have far more flip-flops than a design like this uses.

State machines: Moore, Mealy and the debouncer

    
The one ideaA finite state machine is a state register plus two clouds of combinational logic: one computes the next state, one computes the outputs. In a Moore machine the outputs depend only on the state, so they change only at clock edges. In a Mealy machine they also depend on the inputs, so they react in the same cycle but can pass glitches straight through.

An FSM can be written with one, two or three procedural blocks (slide 12). The lecture uses the common separation: one clocked block for the state register, one combinational block for the next state and outputs, and here a third clocked block that registers the output.


  
The debounce FSM

  
A mechanical switch bounces: for a few milliseconds after it moves, its signal flips back and forth. A debouncer passes on a new level only once it has been stable for long enough. The lecture’s version (slides 14–18) needs two consecutive equal samples:

  

// Lecture 3, slides 15-17: Moore debounce FSM with registered output
module debounce_fsm (
  input  clk,    // clock
  input  reset,  // async reset
  input  din,    // input data
  output dout    // debounced data
);
  // FSM states
  parameter RCV_ZERO    = 2'b00; // received a 1'b0
  parameter RCV_ONE     = 2'b01; // received a 1'b1
  parameter STABLE_ZERO = 2'b10; // stable 1'b0
  parameter STABLE_ONE  = 2'b11; // stable 1'b1

  // Reg and Wire declarations
  reg [1:0] state_ff;   // flip-flops holding the FSM current state
  reg [1:0] next_state; // FSM next state, combinational logic
  reg       dout_i;     // combinational FSM output
  reg       dout_ff;    // registered dout

  // clocked procedure
  always @(posedge clk or posedge reset) begin
    if (reset) begin
      state_ff <= RCV_ONE;
    end else begin
      state_ff <= next_state;
    end
  end

  // combinational procedure
  always @(*) begin // equivalent to always @(din or state_ff)
    case (state_ff)
      STABLE_ZERO: begin
        dout_i = 1'b0;
        if (din) next_state = RCV_ONE;
        else     next_state = STABLE_ZERO;
      end
      STABLE_ONE: begin
        dout_i = 1'b1;
        if (din) next_state = STABLE_ONE;
        else     next_state = RCV_ZERO;
      end
      RCV_ONE: begin
        dout_i = 1'b0;
        if (din) next_state = STABLE_ONE;
        else     next_state = STABLE_ZERO;
      end
      default: begin // RCV_ZERO
        dout_i = 1'b1;
        if (din) next_state = STABLE_ONE;
        else     next_state = STABLE_ZERO;
      end
    endcase
  end

  // registered output
  always @(posedge clk or posedge reset) begin
    if (reset) dout_ff <= 0;
    else       dout_ff <= dout_i;
  end

  // output assignment
  assign dout = dout_ff;
endmodule

  
    
    
  • The four parameter lines (lines 10–13 on slide 15) give each state a 2-bit binary code, so the code reads in state names.
    • 
    
  • The clocked block is the state register, with an asynchronous reset into RCV_ONE. (Resetting into STABLE_ZERO would also be reasonable. From RCV_ONE the first sample decides where the machine settles, and the output is 0 either way.)
    • 
    
  • The combinational block is always @(*) with a case on the state. Each branch assigns both dout_i and next_state on every path, so there are no latches, and default covers the fourth state. dout_i depends only on state_ff: this is a Moore machine.
    • 
    
  • The output register (slide 17) makes dout come straight from a flip-flop: glitch-free, at the cost of one more cycle of delay.
    • 
  

  
Vivado’s schematic of it (slide 18) shows exactly those pieces: multiplexers (RTL_MUX) for the next-state and output logic, the 2-bit state register and the output register, both with asynchronous clear or preset (RTL_REG_ASYNC).

Signed numbers and arithmetic

    
The one ideaVerilog stores negative numbers in two’s complement. The adder hardware is the same for signed and unsigned numbers; what changes is how narrower operands are widened. Declare operands signed and they are sign-extended; let a single unsigned operand into an expression and the whole expression is treated as unsigned.

  


  
Signed types

  
Verilog-1995 had one signed type, the 32-bit integer. Verilog-2001 lets any reg, wire or port be declared signed, and adds the s specifier for signed literals such as 4'sb1101 (slide 20). Arithmetic is everywhere in this course’s applications, from FIR filters to the project’s neural network (slide 22).


  
Two’s complement

  
In an n-bit two’s complement number the most significant bit has weight −2n−1 instead of +2n−1. To negate a number, invert every bit and add 1 (slide 21).

An n-bit signed number covers −2n−1 to 2n−1 − 1, so three bits hold −4 to 3.

      
Example: −3 in 3 bits. 3 = 011 → invert: 100 → add 1: 101. Check: −4 + 0 + 1 = −3.

      
Sign extension: to widen a signed number, copy its sign bit into the new positions. −3 = 101 becomes 1101 in four bits and 1111_1101 in eight. Padding with zeros instead would turn it into +5 (0101, or 0000_0101).

Addition needs one more bit

  
Adding two n-bit numbers can produce an (n+1)-bit result, and signed operands must be sign-extended to that width first (slide 23). The slide’s example adds −2 and 3 as 3-bit signed numbers:

    + 0011   (+3)
    ------

Multiplication needs twice the bits

  
Multiplying two n-bit numbers gives up to 2n bits (slide 24). −3 × 2 should give −6, which is 6'b111010:

  multiplier   +2 = 010

      000000   (bit 0 of 010 is 0)
      111010   (bit 1: −3 shifted left once)
      000000   (bit 2 is 0)
      ------
      111010   = −6

The second method is the slide’s “invert 2, add 1”: because the multiplier’s top bit is worth −4, its partial product is subtracted, which means adding its two’s complement.

The golden rules

  
Slides 25–26
    
    
      
  • If any operand in an operation is unsigned, the entire operation is unsigned.
    • 
      
  • Signed operands are sign-extended to the size of the largest operand (in practice, to the width of the whole expression, including the left-hand side).
    • 
      
  • $unsigned(x) makes the operation unsigned; the operand is then extended with 0s.
    • 
      
  • $signed(x) makes the operand signed; it is then extended with copies of its top bit. To stop an unsigned value becoming negative, pad a 0 on the front first: $signed({1'b0, x}).
    • 
      
  • Verilog leans towards unsigned. These are all unsigned: based numbers without s (4'b1101), bit-selects, part-selects, concatenations, and any operation unless both operands are signed. A plain decimal number (-5, 12) is signed.
    • 
    

  

  
Two simulated examples of the first rule going wrong:


wire signed [3:0] sa = -4'sd3;          // 4'b1101
wire        [3:0] ub = 4'd2;            // unsigned
wire signed [4:0] s1 = sa + ub;         // 15, not -1: ub makes it unsigned, sa is zero-extended
wire signed [4:0] s2 = sa + $signed({1'b0, ub});   // -1, as intended

wire signed [7:0]  x  = -8'sd5;
wire signed [15:0] p1 = x * 4'b0011;    // 753, not -15: the literal is unsigned
wire signed [15:0] p2 = x * 4'sb0011;   // -15

  
The declared signed on the left-hand side does not rescue it: signedness is decided by the operands, before the result is stored.

Arrays, memories and coding rules

    
The one ideaA memory is an array of words: reg [W-1:0] mem [0:D-1] holds D words of W bits. Dimensions written before the name are packed (contiguous bits you can treat as one number); dimensions after the name are unpacked (separate elements you index one at a time). The lecture closes Verilog with seven coding rules that keep RTL synthesisable.

  


  
Arrays in Verilog and SystemVerilog

  
Verilog-2001 allows multi-dimensional arrays as internal variables, for example to model a memory; SystemVerilog extends them to ports, so a module can have output [3:0] mem [1:0] (slide 28).

  

    

      
Unpacked: an array of separate words


reg [3:0] mem_ff [0:3];   // 4 words x 4 bits
// mem_ff[2]    one 4-bit word
// mem_ff[2][1] bit 1 of word 2

      
The packed range [3:0] is the width of each word; the unpacked range [0:3] after the name is the number of words, which is what an address selects. The elements may not be stored as one contiguous block of bits (slide 30), so you cannot compare or assign the whole array as a single number.

    

    

      
Packed: one vector split into fields


reg [3:0][3:0] mem_ff;    // 16 contiguous bits
// mem_ff[3]  bits 15..12
// mem_ff[0]  bits 3..0
// mem_ff == 16'hA5F0 is legal

      
Packed arrays (SystemVerilog) are guaranteed to be one contiguous set of bits (slide 29): a vector with named sub-fields. A one-dimensional packed array is simply a vector; a single bit declared without a range is a scalar.

    

  

  
  
Slide inconsistency
Slides 28–30 draw four words (addresses 2'b00 to 2'b11, mem_ff[3] down to mem_ff[0]) but declare reg [3:0] mem_ff [1:0] and reg [1:0][3:0] mem_ff. A [1:0] dimension has only two entries. For the four words shown you need [3:0] (or [0:3]) in that dimension, as used above.


  
Describing a memory

  
The same pattern describes any RAM: an unpacked array of words, written on a clock edge.


reg [15:0] mem [0:127];            // 128 words of 16 bits: 7 address bits

always @(posedge clk) begin
  if (we)
    mem[addr] <= din;              // synchronous write
  dout <= mem[addr];               // registered (synchronous) read
end

  
On an FPGA the tools decide where this goes. A registered read lets them use block RAM (the 630 KB on the PYNQ-Z2) or, for a small memory like this 2-kbit one, distributed RAM built from LUTs. A read with no register (assign dout = mem[addr];) can only go into LUTs. Synthesising Winter 2024 Q2(c)’s memory for the same FPGA family (with the open-source Yosys tool) confirms it: 16 LUT-RAM primitives plus 16 output flip-flops.


  
Coding rules for synthesis

  
Slide 31, with the reason for each
    
    
      
  • One procedural block per “cloud” of combinational logic, so each block is small and describes one thing.
    • 
      
  • Blocking = in combinational logic, so later statements see earlier results, as the gates would (Lesson 8).
    • 
      
  • Each variable is assigned in one process only. Two always blocks driving the same variable is a race in simulation and a multiple-driver error in synthesis.
    • 
      
  • Every variable gets a value on every path, to avoid latches (Lesson 6).
    • 
      
  • Use _ff for registered signals (and _nxt or _i for their combinational inputs), so a reader can see at a glance which signals are flip-flops.
    • 
      
  • A clocked block’s event list holds one clock and, at most, one reset.
    • 
      
  • Clocked blocks use non-blocking <= and are meant to infer flip-flops.
    • 
    

  

  
Comments (slide 32): // to the end of the line, /* … */ for blocks, which cannot be nested. Colleagues will read and debug your code, so the best comments explain why you did something, your reasoning and the edge cases, not what the line already says. This matters for marks: lab and project code must be “commented thoroughly”.

Self-checking testbenches

    
The one ideaA testbench is a module with no ports. It instantiates the design under test (the DUT), generates a clock, drives stimulus into the inputs, and compares the outputs with a model of what they should be, so the simulation ends by printing PASS or FAIL without anyone reading waveforms.

  


  
What a testbench does

  
It verifies that the design meets its specification (slide 34). The design under test is conventionally named dut. Stimulus can be random, constrained random, or directed at chosen values: every combination, the combinations that stress the block (minimum and maximum of the data range), or real use cases.

  
  
A self-checking testbench decides pass or fail automatically and writes a log (slide 36). It needs no eyeballing of waveforms, is not prone to human error, scales to many inputs and outputs, and can run unattended as a regression after every code change. It is only as good as its test vectors and its predicted outputs. Code coverage measures what the vectors actually exercised: lines, conditions, toggles, FSM states and transitions.

The lecture’s counter testbench

  
Slides 42–46 put it together: the gate-level counter from Lesson 9 is the DUT and the one-line count_ff + 1 version is the predicted model. Both see the same clock and reset, and their outputs are compared every cycle.


`timescale 1ns/1ps
// Lecture 3, slides 43-44: self-checking testbench
module counter_tb;
  // Test-bench variables
  reg        clk;
  reg        reset;
  wire [2:0] count_dut;
  wire [2:0] count_pred;
  integer    pass_cnt;
  integer    err_cnt;

  // Device under test
  counter_gate dut (.clk(clk), .reset(reset), .count(count_dut));
  // Predicted model
  counter predicted_model (.clk(clk), .reset(reset), .count(count_pred));

  // Stimulus
  initial begin
    clk      = 0;
    reset    = 1;
    pass_cnt = 0;
    err_cnt  = 0;
    #50 reset = 0;
    repeat (16) @(negedge clk);
    $display("Simulation completed at", $time);
    if (err_cnt == 0) begin
      $display("Test Pass");
    end else begin
      $display("Test Fail");
    end
    $finish;
  end

  // Clock generation
  always
    #20 clk = ~clk;

  // Monitoring
  always @(negedge clk) begin
    if (count_dut == count_pred) begin
      $display("counter output matched predicted model at", $time, ": expected: %d, actual: %d", count_pred, count_dut);
      pass_cnt <= pass_cnt + 1;
    end else begin
      $display("counter output mismatch at", $time, ": expected: %d, actual: %d", count_pred, count_dut);
      err_cnt <= err_cnt + 1;
    end
  end
endmodule

  
    
    
  • Clock: #20 clk = ~clk toggles every 20 ns, so the period is 40 ns (25 MHz).
    • 
    
  • Reset: held for the first 50 ns, then 16 falling edges later the run stops.
    • 
    
  • Checks at the falling edge. The counters change on the rising edge; sampling half a period later means every output has settled and nothing races with the update.
    • 
    
  • Counts passes and errors and prints the verdict. Compiled and run here, it reports a match at every falling edge, then “Test Pass”, finishing at 680 ns, the same as the console on slide 46.

A three-sample majority filter

Show solution

      
The register holds the last three samples; the vote is the classic majority function: any two of three.


// Practice twin for Lab 3: majority vote of the last three samples
module majority3 (
  input  Clk,
  input  din,     // one new sample every clock
  output vote     // 1 when at least two of the last three samples were 1
);
  wire [2:0] last3;                       // the three most recent samples

  shift_register #(.D_W(3)) u_sr (        // reuse the Lab 3 register, 3 bits wide
    .Clk        (Clk),
    .ShiftIn    (din),
    .ParallelIn (3'b000),                 // parallel load not used
    .load       (1'b0),
    .ShiftEn    (1'b1),                   // shift every clock
    .ShiftOut   (),                       // not needed
    .RegContent (last3)
  );

  assign vote = (last3[0] & last3[1]) | (last3[1] & last3[2]) | (last3[0] & last3[2]);
endmodule

      
The testbench keeps its own copy of the last three samples as the reference model and checks vote just after every rising edge. Run in Icarus Verilog, it prints “PASS: 20 samples checked”.


`timescale 1ns/1ps
module majority3_tb;
  reg         Clk = 1'b0;
  reg         din = 1'b0;
  wire        vote;
  reg  [2:0]  hist = 3'b000;               // testbench's own copy of the last three samples
  reg  [19:0] stream = 20'b0110_1110_0010_1101_0011;
  integer     i, errors = 0;

  majority3 dut (.Clk(Clk), .din(din), .vote(vote));

  always #10 Clk = ~Clk;                   // 50 MHz

  initial begin
    // pre-fill the register with three 0s so the output is defined
    repeat (3) @(negedge Clk);
    for (i = 19; i >= 0; i = i - 1) begin
      @(negedge Clk) din = stream[i];     // new sample on the falling edge
      @(posedge Clk) hist = {hist[1:0], din};
      #1 if (vote !== ((hist[0]&hist[1]) | (hist[1]&hist[2]) | (hist[0]&hist[2]))) begin
        errors = errors + 1;
        $display("mismatch at %0t: history=%b vote=%b", $time, hist, vote);
      end
    end
    if (errors == 0) $display("PASS: 20 samples checked");
    else             $display("FAIL: %0d errors", errors);
    $finish;
  end
endmodule

P2 · Winter 2022 · Q1(b)
Blocking and non-blocking assignments
Lesson 8 · 5 marks

    
Define blocking and non-blocking assignments in Verilog.

    
Show solution

      
    
        
  • Blocking (=): executed immediately, in the order written within a procedural block; the next statement is blocked until it completes, so later statements see the new value. Used for combinational logic (always @(*)).
    • 
        
  • Non-blocking (<=): the right-hand side is evaluated when the statement runs, but the variable is updated only at the end of the time step, after all triggered blocks have been evaluated, so all non-blocking updates on a clock edge appear to happen simultaneously and the result does not depend on statement order. Used for sequential logic (always @(posedge clk)).
    • 
      

      
Example: in a clocked block, q1 = d; q2 = q1; gives q2 = d (one stage), while q1 <= d; q2 <= q1; gives a two-stage shift register (q2 gets the old q1). Rule: never mix the two in the same block.

    

  


  

    
P3 · 2022 Q1(d) · 2024 Q1(h) · 2025 Q1(b)
Mealy and Moore machines
Lesson 10 · 5 marks, every year

    
Define, and draw a diagram (schematic) of, the Mealy and Moore finite state machines.

    
Show solution

      
    
        
  • An FSM is a sequential circuit with a finite number of states: a state register, combinational next-state logic and combinational output logic.
    • 
        
  • Moore: outputs depend only on the current state. Outputs change only after a clock edge (one cycle after the input that caused them), so input glitches cannot reach them and they are safer to connect onward (they can still glitch briefly while several state bits change, unless the outputs are registered), but may need more states.
    • 
        
  • Mealy: outputs depend on the current state and the inputs. It reacts in the same cycle (lower latency) and usually needs fewer states, but input glitches reach the output, and connecting two Mealy machines can create a combinational loop.
    • 
      

      
Draw the two diagrams from Lesson 10: Moore = inputs → next-state logic → state register → output logic → outputs, with the state fed back; Mealy = inputs and state → one combinational block → next state (to the register) and outputs. Label the clock on the state register.

    

  


  

    
P4 · Winter 2025 · Q1(h)
Two state encodings for 11 states
Lessons 9, 10 · 5 marks

    
Present two state encoding schemes, their advantages and drawbacks, in the context of finite state machines with 11 states.

    
Show solution

      
    
        
  • Binary: states numbered 0000 to 1010, so ⌈log₂ 11⌉ = 4 flip-flops. For: fewest registers. Against: next-state and output logic must decode 4-bit codes (more logic levels, slower); several bits can flip on one transition (glitches, more switching power); 5 unused codes must be handled so the machine cannot get stuck.
    • 
        
  • One-hot: one flip-flop per state, exactly one set: 11 flip-flops. For: very simple next-state logic (each flop is a small OR of incoming transitions), outputs are single bits or small ORs, so it is fast and easy to modify; suits FPGAs, which have plenty of flip-flops. Against: nearly three times the registers, and 2¹¹ − 11 = 2,037 illegal codes, so a glitch can leave zero or two bits set unless recovery logic is added.
    • 
      

      
A third option worth one line: Gray encoding (also 4 flip-flops), where consecutive states differ in one bit (with 11 states, every step except the wrap from the last state back to the first), reducing glitches and power when the machine steps through states in order.

Binary literals and two’s-complement addition

Represent in Verilog using binary notation the decimal numbers −8 and 11. Compute by hand the result of adding −13 to −17 using two’s complement.

    
Show solution

      
−8: 8 = 1000, invert 0111, add 1 → 1000. Four signed bits are enough (range −8 to 7): 4'sb1000, or sign-extended to a byte, 8'sb1111_1000.

      
11: 4'b1011 as an unsigned number. As a signed number it needs five bits (4 signed bits stop at 7): 5'sb01011.

      
−13 + (−17) = −30. Six bits are needed (range −32 to 31).

      
13 = 001101 → invert 110010 → +1 → 110011  (−13)
17 = 010001 → invert 101110 → +1 → 101111  (−17)

    110011
  + 101111
  --------
   1100010   carry out of bit 5 is discarded
    100010   = −32 + 2 = −30

      
No overflow: both operands are negative and so is the result. (Overflow happens only when two numbers of the same sign give a result of the other sign.) Checked in simulation: a 6-bit signed sum of −13 and −17 is 100010.

  input        clk,
  input        reset,
  output [2:0] count
);
  reg [2:0] count_ff;
  always @(posedge clk or posedge reset) begin
    if (reset) begin
      count_ff <= 3'b0;
    end else begin
      count_ff <= count_ff + 1'b1;
    end
  end
  assign count = count_ff;
endmodule

      
(c) One-hot uses one flip-flop per state: 8 flip-flops, exactly one holding 1. The next-state logic disappears: each flip-flop’s D is the previous flip-flop’s Q, a ring (Q7 feeds D0). Reset must preset the state-0 flop to 1 and clear the other seven. If a binary count output is still needed, three OR gates encode it: count[0] = S1+S3+S5+S7, count[1] = S2+S3+S6+S7, count[2] = S4+S5+S6+S7. The one-hot and binary versions were simulated side by side for 20 cycles with no mismatch.

Show solution

      
(a) One combinational block with a case. The 9-bit concatenation {carry, res} catches the carry of the addition and the bit shifted out of a; the default carry = 0 covers the logic operations and prevents a latch.


// Winter 2022 Q2(a): behavioural ALU (combinational)
module alu (
  input      [2:0] op_code,
  input      [7:0] a,
  input      [7:0] b,
  output reg [7:0] res,
  output reg       carry
);
  always @(*) begin
    carry = 1'b0;                          // default: no carry
    case (op_code)
      3'd0:    {carry, res} = a + b;         // unsigned add, 9-bit result
      3'd1:    {carry, res} = {a, 1'b0};     // shift left by 1: a[7] -> carry
      3'd2:    res = a & b;                  // bitwise and
      3'd3:    res = a | b;                  // bitwise or
      3'd4:    res = (a < b) ? 8'd1 : 8'd0;  // less than
      default: {carry, res} = a + b;         // unsigned add
    endcase
  end
endmodule

      
(b) Register op_enable once, so that op_enable & ~op_enable_ff is 1 for exactly the first cycle after it rises. On that edge, capture the ALU result and set op_rdy; one edge later op_rdy falls again. Reusing the part (a) module as an instance keeps the answer short.


// Winter 2022 Q2(b): registered ALU with op_enable / op_rdy handshake
module alu_seq (
  input            clk,
  input            op_enable,   // rising edge = request a new operation
  input      [2:0] op_code,
  input      [7:0] a,
  input      [7:0] b,
  output reg [7:0] res,
  output reg       carry,
  output reg       op_rdy       // one-cycle pulse when res/carry are valid
);
  reg        op_enable_ff;                     // op_enable one clock ago
  wire       start = op_enable & ~op_enable_ff; // rising edge of op_enable
  wire [7:0] res_nxt;
  wire       carry_nxt;

  // reuse the combinational ALU from part (a)
  alu u_alu (.op_code(op_code), .a(a), .b(b), .res(res_nxt), .carry(carry_nxt));

  always @(posedge clk) begin
    op_enable_ff <= op_enable;
    op_rdy       <= start;          // high for exactly one cycle
    if (start) begin
      res   <= res_nxt;             // capture the result
      carry <= carry_nxt;
    end
  end
endmodule

Show solution

      
(a)

      

      
Inputs: Clk (1), ShiftIn (1), ParallelIn[3:0] (4), load (1), ShiftEn (1). Outputs: ShiftOut (1) = bit 3, RegContent[3:0] (4). load has priority; the register only updates when load or ShiftEn is 1. A bit-level version is the figure in Lesson 7.

(c) 20 MHz means a 50 ns period, so the clock toggles every 25 ns. Inputs change on falling edges, half a period away from the rising edge that samples them.


`timescale 1ns/1ps
// Winter 2022 Q4(c): 20 MHz clock and arbitrary stimulus for shift_register
module shift_register_tb;
  reg        Clk        = 1'b0;
  reg        ShiftIn    = 1'b0;
  reg  [3:0] ParallelIn = 4'b0000;
  reg        load       = 1'b0;
  reg        ShiftEn    = 1'b0;
  wire       ShiftOut;
  wire [3:0] RegContent;

  shift_register #(.D_W(4)) dut (
    .Clk(Clk), .ShiftIn(ShiftIn), .ParallelIn(ParallelIn), .load(load),
    .ShiftEn(ShiftEn), .ShiftOut(ShiftOut), .RegContent(RegContent));

  always #25 Clk = ~Clk;            // 20 MHz: period 50 ns, half-period 25 ns

  initial begin
    // change inputs on falling edges, away from the rising edge where they are sampled
    @(negedge Clk); ParallelIn = 4'b0101; load = 1'b1;   // parallel load 0101
    @(negedge Clk); load = 1'b0; ShiftEn = 1'b1; ShiftIn = 1'b1;
    repeat (3) @(negedge Clk);                           // shift in three 1s
    ShiftIn = 1'b0;
    repeat (2) @(negedge Clk);                           // shift in two 0s
    ShiftEn = 1'b0;                                      // hold
    repeat (2) @(negedge Clk);
    ParallelIn = 4'b1001; load = 1'b1; ShiftEn = 1'b1;  // load wins over ShiftEn
    @(negedge Clk); load = 1'b0;
    repeat (4) @(negedge Clk);
    $finish;
  end

  always @(posedge Clk)
    #1 $display("t=%0d ns load=%b ShiftEn=%b ShiftIn=%b | RegContent=%b ShiftOut=%b",
                $time, load, ShiftEn, ShiftIn, RegContent, ShiftOut);
endmodule

A linear feedback shift register and its testbench
Lessons 7, 13 · 30 marks

    

      
Figure 3.1 shows an LFSR with characteristic polynomial x⁵ + x³ + x + 1: five rising-edge D flip-flops with outputs X0–X4. X4 is fed back into three XOR gates, in front of flip-flops 1, x and x³; the serial input 01010001 enters through the XOR in front of flip-flop 1. Inputs: INP, CLOCK, RESET.

      
    
        
  1. Write the synthesisable Verilog for this circuit, assuming an active-high asynchronous reset; at reset the flip-flops are all set to 1. [15]
    2. 
        
  2. Present a testbench which creates a 100 MHz CLOCK (logic 0 at 0 ns), an asynchronous active-high reset (logic 1 for 20 ns starting at 0 ns), and the input and output as in the figure. The serial input is available at the falling edge of the clock. [15]
    3. 
      

    

    
Show solution

      
(a) Read each flip-flop’s D input off the figure: X0 ← INP ⊕ X4, X1 ← X0 ⊕ X4, X2 ← X1, X3 ← X2 ⊕ X4, X4 ← X3. All five update on the same edge, so non-blocking assignments read the old values, exactly as the hardware does.


// Winter 2025 Q3(a): LFSR of Figure 3.1, characteristic polynomial x^5 + x^3 + x + 1
module lfsr (
  input            CLOCK,
  input            RESET,   // active-high, asynchronous
  input            INP,     // serial input
  output reg [4:0] X        // X[0] .. X[4] = the five flip-flop outputs
);
  always @(posedge CLOCK or posedge RESET) begin
    if (RESET) begin
      X <= 5'b11111;              // at reset all flip-flops are 1
    end else begin
      X[0] <= INP  ^ X[4];        // XOR in front of flip-flop "1"
      X[1] <= X[0] ^ X[4];        // XOR in front of flip-flop "x"
      X[2] <= X[1];               // plain shift
      X[3] <= X[2] ^ X[4];        // XOR in front of flip-flop "x^3"
      X[4] <= X[3];               // plain shift; X[4] is the feedback
    end
  end
endmodule

      
(b) 100 MHz is a 10 ns period: toggle every 5 ns from 0. Falling edges are then at 10, 20, 30 ns…; reset falls at 20 ns, so the first input bit goes on at 20 ns and each bit is held for one period. A 128 × 16-bit memory
Lesson 12 · 10 marks

    
Describe in Verilog a 128 × 16 bit memory with inputs ADDR (address), Data_in and output Data_out. An RW input signal should indicate read from memory when 1 and write to memory when 0.

    
Show solution

      
128 words need a 7-bit address (2⁷ = 128); each word is 16 bits. The question lists no clock, but a synthesisable RAM is synchronous, so add one and say so.


// Winter 2024 Q2(c): 128 x 16-bit memory; RW = 1 read, RW = 0 write
module mem128x16 (
  input             clk,
  input             RW,         // 1 = read, 0 = write
  input      [6:0]  ADDR,       // 128 words -> 7 address bits
  input      [15:0] Data_in,
  output reg [15:0] Data_out
);
  reg [15:0] mem [0:127];       // 128 words of 16 bits

  always @(posedge clk) begin
    if (RW == 1'b0)
      mem[ADDR] <= Data_in;     // write
    else
      Data_out  <= mem[ADDR];   // read (registered, so it maps to block RAM)
  end
endmodule

      
    
        
  • reg [15:0] mem [0:127]: packed width 16, unpacked depth 128 (Lesson 12).
    • 
        
  • Write (RW = 0) and read (RW = 1) both happen on the rising edge; the read is registered, which lets the FPGA tools use block RAM or LUT RAM.
    • 
        
  • A testbench that writes all 128 words and reads them back found no errors.

Definitions

      
VLSI: millions–billions of gates on one chip. FPGA: configurable LUTs, flip-flops, BRAM, DSP and routing, set by a bitstream. EDA: design-automation software (simulate, synthesise, place and route, STA, power). Standard cell: pre-characterised gate, fixed height, in rows. HDL: language describing hardware for simulation and synthesis.

      
Moore (1965): components at minimum cost double every year (later two). Dennard (1974): power density constant under scaling; ended ~2005 (voltage, leakage) → power wall, dark silicon.

Flip-flops

always @(posedge clk)

always @(posedge clk or posedge reset)

FSM, three blocks


always @(posedge clk or posedge reset)
  if (reset) state_ff <= S0;
  else       state_ff <= next_state;

// next state and outputs
always @(*) begin
  next_state = state_ff;  // defaults:
  out_i      = 1'b0;      // no latches
  case (state_ff)
    S0: if (in) next_state = S1;
    S1: begin
      out_i      = 1'b1;
      next_state = S0;
    end
  endcase
end

always @(posedge clk) out_ff <= out_i;

      
Moore: outputs from state only. Mealy: from state and inputs. N states: binary ⌈log₂N⌉ flip-flops, one-hot N.

    

    
Numbers

      
n-bit signed: −2n−1 … 2n−1−1. Negate: invert, add 1. Widen: copy the sign bit.

      
n + n → n+1 bits. n × n → 2n bits.

      
Any unsigned operand ⇒ whole expression unsigned. Based literals, bit/part-selects and concatenations are unsigned; plain decimals are signed. 4'sb1101 = −3; 4'd20 truncates to 4'b0100.
