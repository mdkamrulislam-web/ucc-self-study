The big picture

The one ideaAnalogue circuits process continuous, real-world signals: they amplify and filter them as cleanly as possible. The CMOS inverter you know from digital becomes an amplifier when you bias both transistors “half on”.

Analogue and digital, side by side

AnalogueDigital
SignalContinuous in amplitude and time, e.g. a sine waveTwo levels (0 and 1), changing at clock edges
What mattersGain, frequency response, signal-to-noise ratio, linearity, offsetNumber of bits, speed
RobustnessGain drifts with temperature, ageing and manufacturing spreadAn inverter always inverts
PowerA bias current flows all the timeCurrent flows mainly while switching

Why 1.5 V? The NMOS needs its gate about 0.7 V above its source (ground) to turn on. The PMOS needs its gate about 0.7 V below its source (3 V), so below 2.3 V. Half the supply satisfies both. The output is also biased near half the supply so the signal has equal room to swing up and down.

Once both transistors are on, the questions the course asks are the ones in the table: how much gain can you get and what does it cost in power, how fast can it go (every resistance and capacitance makes a pole that filters the signal), how much noise do the transistors add, how linear is the output, and (for op-amps) how much offset is there.

Why analogue still matters

Nature is analogue: microphones, radio waves, image sensors and temperature sensors all deliver weak continuous signals. A typical chip amplifies and filters them, converts to digital (ADC), processes digitally, converts back (DAC) and drives an output. Analogue designers also build the clock generators (oscillators and phase-locked loops with low jitter) and the input/output drivers. Circuits that cross between the two worlds are called mixed-signal.

digital is the brain; analogue is the senses and the muscles.

What makes integrated circuit design different

  • One process, fixed menu. You design for a specific foundry process with its own transistors, maximum supply voltage, resistors and capacitors.
  • Everything spreads. Threshold voltages and resistor values vary between wafers and batches, and with supply voltage and temperature. Designs must work across all of this: PVT (process, voltage, temperature).
  • Hand analysis first, then simulation. Most of the course is “back of the envelope” analysis with a simple transistor model. The labs then simulate the same circuits in Cadence (Spectre simulator, BSIM transistor models) in a 0.35 µm, 3.3 V process from AMS.

A real example: battery management

The lecturer’s group at Infineon in Cork designs battery-management chips. An electric-vehicle battery is a stack of lithium-ion cells of about 4 V each. The state of charge is read from each cell’s voltage, but the voltage-versus-charge curve is very flat: 1% of charge is only about 3 mV. So the whole measurement chain needs roughly 3 mV total error. That error budget is made of gain error, noise, offset (transistor mismatch), non-linearity, the accuracy of the reference voltage (a bandgap) and the temperature sensor. Those are exactly the topics of the later sections.

Levels of abstraction and the course map

Analogue design is described at four levels: device (the transistor), circuit, architecture and system. This course is mainly at the circuit level: start from the transistor, build gain stages, then op-amps. The 27 sections run: MOS review (1–4) → single-transistor stages (5–13) → current mirrors and matching (14–15) → differential amplifiers (16–17) → frequency response, feedback and compensation (18–21) → noise (22–23) → op-amps (24–25) → references (26) → active filters (27). So far (to 25 September): sections 1–8 complete.

  • 1965: first analogue IC op-amp, the µA709 (Widlar, Fairchild); 1968: µA741 with the compensation capacitor on chip. Moore’s law predicted (1965).

  • 2000s on: RF CMOS radios, smartphones full of sensors, IoT. Around 22 nm, planar transistors give way to FinFETs.

Inside a MOSFET

The one ideaA MOSFET is a voltage-controlled valve. The gate is one plate of a capacitor; put enough voltage on it and a thin layer of electrons (the channel) forms underneath, connecting source to drain. The rest of this lesson (wells, bulk connections) is about keeping millions of these valves isolated from each other.

Two jobs: switch or amplifier

Digital circuits use the transistor as a switch: fully on or fully off. Analogue circuits keep it on and use the gate voltage to control the current smoothly, as an amplifier. The questions from slide 34 frame everything that follows: at what gate voltage does it turn on, how much current (or resistance) do you get, what does that depend on, and how fast can it respond?

The NMOS, layer by layer

  • Bulk (substrate), B: lightly doped p-type silicon. This is the fourth terminal.
  • Source and drain: two heavily doped n+ islands. The device is symmetric; for an NMOS the source is whichever end sits at the lower voltage.
  • Gate oxide: a very thin insulator (about 7.5 nm in the lab process).
  • Gate: polysilicon today; the “M” in MOS is from when gates were metal.
  • L is the channel length, the distance the electrons travel from source to drain. W is the width, into the page.

Why the bulk connection matters: isolation

Every n+ island sitting in p-type silicon is a pn diode. Tie the p-substrate to the most negative voltage on the chip (ground) and all of those diodes are reverse-biased: they never conduct, so each transistor is electrically isolated from its neighbours. That is how millions of devices can share one piece of silicon. PMOS transistors sit in n-wells, and for the same reason each n-well is tied to the most positive voltage (VDD).

Turning it on

  1. Small gate voltage: the positive gate pushes the holes away, leaving a depletion region of fixed negative ions. There are no free electrons, so no current. The gate just sees two capacitors in series: the oxide and the depletion layer.
  2. Gate above Vt: enough electrons gather under the oxide to form a thin conducting layer, the channel (also called the inversion layer, because the p-type surface now behaves as n-type).
  3. Add a drain voltage: electrons flow from source to drain, so the conventional current ID flows from drain to source.
Where the threshold voltage comes from (slide 52, not needed for calculations)

Vt is defined as the gate-source voltage at which the electron concentration at the surface equals the hole concentration deep in the substrate, i.e. the surface potential has changed by 2ϕF where ϕF=kTqln⁡NAni. Adding up the work-function difference, the surface potential and the depletion charge gives

Vt=ϕMS+2ϕF+QdepCox

In practice the quoted Vt is measured from the I–V curve, and you are always given it.

The PMOS: everything flipped

A PMOS has p+ source and drain inside an n-well. A gate more than |Vtp| below the source attracts holes and forms a p-type channel. The source is usually at VDD, and current flows from source to drain. Holes move about three times more slowly than electrons (lower mobility), so a PMOS gives roughly 3–4× less current than an NMOS of the same size. The course uses a factor of 4.

CMOS: both on one chip

  • Standard n-well CMOS: NMOS directly in the p-substrate (all sharing it); PMOS in n-wells tied to VDD. One n-well can hold many PMOS.
  • Deep n-well (triple well): an isolated p-well lets an NMOS tie its bulk to its own source. That removes the body effect (Lesson 5) and shields sensitive analogue circuits from substrate noise. It costs an extra mask and area, and the well junctions still couple a little through their capacitance.
  • Silicon-on-insulator (SOI): transistors sit on a buried oxide layer. Best isolation, lower junction capacitance, immune to latch-up; used for high-performance RF; more expensive.

Schematic versus layout

You design with schematics, but what goes to the foundry is layout: the polygons for diffusion, polysilicon, contacts and metal. Layout is a specialist job. After layout the circuit is extracted and simulated again to check nothing changed. One detail: the source and drain diffuse slightly under the gate, so the effective length is a little shorter than the drawn length; the course always uses the drawn L.

Shrinking L and tox gives more, faster transistors (Moore’s law: roughly double every two years). But a shorter channel punches through at a lower drain voltage and a thinner oxide breaks down at a lower gate voltage, so VDD had to fall. High-k gate dielectrics keep the gate capacitance up with a physically thicker layer.

Below about 22 nm, planar transistors ran out of steam. A FinFET wraps the gate around a vertical fin of silicon. Its width is fixed by the fin, W=2Hfin+Tfin, so to get a wider transistor you put fins in parallel: W comes in steps. The newest nodes use gate-all-around stacks. Reassuringly, the same I–V equations still hold.

For analogue, scaling is a mixed blessing. A lower supply means a smaller signal swing, while the transistors’ noise stays about the same, so the signal-to-noise ratio gets worse, and short transistors have low gain. That is why chips keep thicker-oxide 2.5 V, 3.3 V or 5 V transistors for analogue blocks, and why a lot of analogue design still uses 0.35 µm, 3.3 V processes like the one in the labs.

The I–V equation

The one ideaDrain current = (charge in the channel) × (how fast it moves). With a small VDS the channel behaves like a resistor. Raise VDS and the channel pinches off at the drain end: the current stops growing and depends only on VGS. That flat region, called saturation, is where analogue circuits live.

The lecturer’s advice: don’t worry about every line of the derivation, it’s the outcome that matters. Here it is in four short steps so the outcome makes sense.

Step 1 · How much charge is in the channel?

The gate and the channel form a capacitor, and charge = capacitance × voltage. Only the gate voltage above threshold creates channel charge, so

Q=WLCox(VGS−Vt)

where Cox=εox/tox is the gate capacitance per unit area and WL is the gate area. The quantity VGS−Vt appears everywhere in this course. It is the overdrive voltage, written VOV (you may also see VGT or Veff).

Step 2 · Add a drain voltage

With the source at 0 V and the drain at VDS, the voltage inside the channel, V(x), rises from 0 at the source to VDS at the drain. At position x the gate only “sees” VGS−V(x), so the channel is thinner towards the drain. Per metre of channel:

Qd(x)=WCox(VGS−V(x)−Vt)

Step 3 · Current = charge × speed

Electrons drift at speed v=μnE, where μn is their mobility and the field is the voltage gradient E=dV/dx. Current is charge per metre times metres per second:

ID=μnCoxW(VGS−V(x)−Vt)dVdx

The current is the same all along the channel, so multiply both sides by dx and integrate from the source (x=0, V=0) to the drain (x=L, V=VDS):

ID=μnCoxWL[(VGS−Vt)VDS−VDS22]

This is the triode (or linear) equation: a parabola in VDS.

Step 4 · The parabola peaks: pinch-off

Differentiate with respect to VDS and set it to zero: the peak is at VDS=VGS−Vt, where the current is 12μnCoxWL(VGS−Vt)2. Past the peak the parabola would come back down, but the real current doesn’t. Here is why.

If VDS>VGS−Vt, then VGD=VGS−VDS<Vt. At the drain end the gate is no longer a threshold above the channel, so the channel pinches off there. Electrons still cross the short pinched-off gap (the strong field sweeps them over), but extra drain voltage just drops across that gap. To first order the current stays at its peak value. This is saturation.

Remember · the square law ID=μnCox2WL(VGS−Vt)2

Three things set the current: the technology (μnCox, fixed by the foundry), the geometry (W/L, your choice) and the bias (VGS−Vt, your choice). Double W/L and the current doubles; double the overdrive and it quadruples.

a waterfall. The flow over the edge is set by the river upstream (VGS), not by how far the water falls (VDS). Making the drop taller doesn’t increase the flow.

A word on names. “Saturation” sounds bad, and for bipolar transistors it means fully switched on. For a MOSFET it means the current has saturated, and that is exactly where an amplifier’s transistors must sit. The triode region is where the transistor behaves like a resistor.

PMOS without the sign headaches

The slides write the PMOS with negative VGS, VDS and Vt. It is much easier to use magnitudes, VSG=−VGS and VSD=−VDS:

  • On if VSG>|Vtp| (the gate is at least |Vtp| below the source).
  • Saturated if VSD≥VSG−|Vtp|.
  • Current (flowing source → drain): ID=Kp′2WL(VSG−|Vtp|)2.

Deep triode: a resistor you can tune (slide 61)

When VDS≪2(VGS−Vt) the squared term is tiny and the current is simply proportional to VDS. The channel is a resistor whose value the gate controls:

RDS=1μnCoxWL(VGS−Vt)

More overdrive, lower resistance; in cut-off, infinite. This is the “on” transistor in a digital gate, and analogue circuits use it for switches, gain control and tunable filters.

Worked example

An NMOS has Kn′=200μA/V2, W/L=10, Vt=0.75V and VGS=1.25V, so VOV=0.5V.

  1. In saturation (any VDS≥0.5V): ID=2002×10×0.52=250μA.
  2. At VDS=0.2V it is in triode: ID=200×10×(0.5×0.2−0.22/2)=2000×0.08=160μA.

Which region is it in?

The one ideaAsk two questions. Is it on? Then: is there still a channel at the drain end? If the gate is not more than one threshold above the drain, the channel is pinched off and the transistor is in saturation.

The two-step test

NMOS
  1. On? VGS>Vt. If not: cut-off.
  2. Channel at the drain? If VGD≤Vt it is pinched off: saturation. Otherwise triode.
PMOS
  1. On? VSG>|Vtp|. If not: cut-off.
  2. Channel at the drain? If VDG≤|Vtp|: saturation. Otherwise triode.

The gate–drain test is the same condition as the textbook one (VDS≥VGS−Vt, just rearranged), but it only needs the gate and drain voltages. That is handy when you don’t know the source voltage, as in stacked transistors below. It is also the physical reason: saturation means there isn’t enough gate voltage at the drain end to hold the channel open.

The homework from slides 62 and 63

Take Vt = 1 V for the NMOS cases and Vt = −1 V for the PMOS cases, bulk tied to source. Decide each region, then open the answers.

Show NMOS answers
  1. VGS = 2 V > 1 V, so on. VGD = 2 − 3 = −1 V, below Vt, so pinched off: saturation. (Textbook check: VDS = 3 V ≥ VGS − Vt = 1 V.)
  2. On. VGD = 2 − 0.5 = 1.5 V, above Vt, so the channel reaches the drain: triode.
  3. VGS = 0.5 V < 1 V: cut-off. Nothing else to check.
  4. Gate tied to drain, so VGD = 0, always below Vt. It is on (VGS = 2 V), so saturation, by construction.
Show PMOS answers
  1. VSG = 5 − 3 = 2 V > 1 V, so on. VDG = 3 − 3 = 0 V, below |Vt|: saturation.
  2. On. VDG = 4.5 − 3 = 1.5 V, above |Vt|, so the channel exists at the drain: triode.
  3. VSG = 0.5 V < 1 V: cut-off.
  4. Diode-connected and on (VSG = 2 V): saturation by construction.

Diode-connected transistors are always saturated

Tie the gate to the drain and VGD=0<Vt. If the transistor conducts at all, it is saturated. It looks like a diode (one terminal pair, current flows one way above a turn-on voltage), hence the name. You will meet it constantly: in bias generators, as loads, and in current mirrors.

A stacked pair (from the lecture on 11 Sept)

Top transistor M2: gate 2.5 V, drain 3 V, so VGD = −0.5 V, below Vt: saturation. Notice we didn’t need its source voltage at all.

Bottom transistor M1: we need VX, and that needs one more fact. If both transistors have the same W/L, then they carry the same current (they are in series), have the same K′ and Vt, so the square law forces the same VGS. M1 has VGS = 1.5 V, so M2 has too, and VX = 2.5 − 1.5 = 1.0 V. Then M1 has VGD = 1.5 − 1.0 = 0.5 V < 1 V: also saturation.

Big idea: in a stack, the middle node sits one VGS below the upper gate, VX = VG2 − VGS2. If M2 had a smaller W/L it would need a larger VGS for the same current, VX would drop and M1 could fall out of saturation. A larger W/L for M2 raises VX.

Second-order effects

The one ideaThe square law is a very good first guess, and real transistors add small corrections. Only one of them matters for numbers in this course: channel-length modulation (λ), because it sets the transistor’s output resistance and therefore the gain.

1 · Channel-length modulation (λ)

In saturation, raising VDS pushes the pinch-off point back towards the source, so the effective channel is shorter by ΔL (third panel of the pinch-off figure in Lesson 3). A shorter channel means a larger W/L and a little more current. Assume ΔL/L=λVDS:

ID=Kn′2WL(VGS−Vt)2(1+λVDS)

A longer transistor notices a given ΔL less, so λ∝1/L. The course uses λ = 0.04/L (L in µm, λ in V⁻¹).

How big is it?

L = 1 µm gives λ = 0.04 V⁻¹. At VDS = 1 V the current is 4% more than the plain square law predicts.

So in saturation the current is a strong function of VGS and a weak function of VDS. Remember that sentence: it is the reason amplifiers work (Lesson 8).

  • DC biasing: use λ = 0. The error is a few percent, hand analysis becomes much easier, and the exam paper tells you to.
  • Gain: keep λ. It gives the transistor its finite output resistance, and without it the gain would be infinite.

2 · Body effect (back-gate effect)

If the source sits above the bulk (VSB>0), the depletion layer under the gate grows, and the gate must supply more charge before a channel forms. The threshold rises:

Vt=Vt0+γ(2ϕF+VSB−2ϕF),γ≈0.3 V (typical)

It is unwanted because the bulk is shared by all the NMOS, so you can’t control it per transistor, and any noise on the substrate modulates Vt. It appears in stacked (cascode) transistors: the upper transistor’s source sits a few hundred mV above ground, so its Vt rises by tens of mV. The fix is an isolated p-well with the bulk tied to the source, at an area cost.

Exam rule: “the body effect may be ignored” unless a question says otherwise. The one problem so far that includes it is slide 126 (Lesson 10).

3 · Weak inversion and subthreshold conduction

The simple model switches off abruptly at Vt. In reality a small diffusion current flows even below threshold, growing exponentially with VGS:

ID≈ID0WLexp⁡(VGSnkT/q),kTq≈26 mV, n≈1.5

with ID0 of the order of tens of nA. For overdrives between 0 and roughly 100–200 mV the transistor is in weak inversion, a smooth transition to the square law. It is used for very low-power circuits, and simulation models can be inaccurate there. Course rule: keep VGS − Vt above about 200 mV (strong inversion) so the square law holds.

4 · Velocity saturation and other short-channel effects

Electrons can’t go faster than about 10⁵ m/s. At high fields the effective mobility drops and the current is lower than μnCox predicts; at large overdrive the current becomes almost linear in VGS − Vt. Short channels also show threshold shifts with L and with VDS (drain-induced barrier lowering) and hot-carrier effects. Using L longer than the minimum reduces all of these, which is common in analogue design.

This is why the course uses measured values Kn′ and Kp′, which are a little smaller than μnCox and μpCox: they absorb these effects. The resulting three-parameter model (K′, Vt, λ) is called the square-law, Shichman–Hodges or Level 1 model. Real simulators use BSIM3 or BSIM4 with many more parameters.

The one ideaBiasing means choosing DC voltages and currents so every transistor is in saturation with the current you want. You need three tools: the square law with λ = 0, the saturation test, and one trick: same current and same W/L means the same VGS.

  2. Use ID=Kn′2WLVOV2 with λ = 0.

Layout note: the cascode and the body effect

The upper transistor M2 in a stack has its source above ground, so its Vt rises a little (body effect) and it needs slightly more headroom. You could put M2 in its own isolated p-well with bulk tied to source, but the wells need generous spacing and cost a lot of area. Left in the substrate, M1 and M2 can share one diffusion (M1’s drain is M2’s source) and the layout is very compact. Usually designers accept the slightly higher Vt.

Two tricks for exam bias questions

These follow directly from the square law and appear in most past papers. (They are the basis of current mirrors, Section 14.)

  • Same VGS, different W/L: two transistors with gates tied together and sources tied together have the same VGS, so their currents are in the ratio of their W/L. A 6.4/1 and a 64/1 carry currents in the ratio 1 : 10.
  • Identical diodes in series: N identical diode-connected transistors in a chain carry the same current, so each has the same VGS and the supply splits equally. Three across 3 V gives 1 V each.

The small-signal model

The one ideaBias the transistor with DC, then wiggle the input a tiny amount. Zoomed in that far, the curved square law looks like a straight line, so the transistor behaves like a simple linear circuit: a current source gmvgs with a resistor 1/gds across it.

Circuit toolkit refresher (slides 86–92)

  • Conductance G=1/R. Resistors in series add; conductances in parallel add. The course prefers conductances because an amplifier’s output node has several things in parallel. Units: the course writes µA/V (the same as µS).
  • Dividers: series resistors share a voltage in proportion to R; parallel conductances share a current in proportion to G.
  • Sources: an ideal current source gives its current whatever the voltage; a real one has a parallel output resistance, so its current rises a little with voltage. An ideal voltage source holds its voltage whatever the current; a real one has a series resistance, so its voltage sags under load.
  • Dependent sources: a voltage-controlled current source, i=gvk, is exactly what a transistor’s gmvgs is. A voltage-controlled voltage source, v=A(vA−vB), is the simplest op-amp model.
  • Kirchhoff’s current law (KCL): currents into a node sum to zero. This is the workhorse for every gain calculation in the course. (KVL: voltages round a loop sum to zero.)

Big signals and small signals

Notation (slide 93): capitals for DC values (VIN, ID), lower case for the small wiggles (vin, id), and the total is the sum, Vin = VIN + vin.

Take the common-source stage with a load resistor: VDD, then RL, then the output, then the transistor to ground, with VIN + vin on the gate. The output is VDD minus the drop across RL, so it splits cleanly:

VOUT=VDD−IDRL(DC)vout=−idRL(signal)

The minus sign is the inversion: more gate voltage, more current, more drop across RL, lower output.

Linearising

The DC bias sets the operating point. Around it, the small-signal parameters are simply the slopes of the large-signal curves. This is valid while (1) the wiggle is small compared with VGS − Vt, and (2) the transistor stays in saturation the whole time. A simulator does exactly the same: a DC analysis finds the operating point, then an AC analysis uses the linearised circuit.

Remember · two conversion rules
  • A DC voltage source (VDD, VBIAS, VIN) can’t wiggle: replace it with a short to ground (“AC ground”).
  • A DC current source can’t wiggle its current: replace it with an open circuit.

gm: how strongly the gate controls the current

The transconductance is the slope of ID against VGS with VDS held fixed. Differentiating the square law gives three equivalent forms (slide 97):

Remember · gm in saturation gm=Kn′WL(VGS−Vt)=2Kn′WLID=2IDVGS−Vt

Pick the one that matches what you know: dimensions and overdrive; dimensions and current; or current and overdrive (the one used most).

In triode gm=Kn′WLVDS; in cut-off it is zero. Neither is used much. A value of 1 mA/V means a 1 mV wiggle on the gate moves the drain current by 1 µA.

gds: how weakly the drain controls the current

The output conductance is the slope of ID against VDS with VGS fixed. From the λ equation:

gds=λKn′2WL(VGS−Vt)2≈λID,rds=1gds

Example: ID = 100 µA and λ = 0.04 V⁻¹ give gds = 4 µA/V, so rds = 250 kΩ.

gmb: the body effect’s contribution

If the source moves relative to the bulk, Vt moves, so the current moves: gmb=ηgm with η between about 0.1 and 0.3. If the source is tied to the bulk, vbs = 0 and you can drop it.

The complete low-frequency model

NMOS and PMOS share the model

(From the 22 Sept lecture.) For an NMOS, raising the gate relative to the source increases the current flowing drain → source. For a PMOS, raising the gate relative to the source reduces the current flowing source → drain. A decrease from source to drain is the same thing as an increase from drain to source. So in both models the gmvgs arrow points from drain to source, and you can use the same model for both.

Exam angle

“Draw the small-signal equivalent circuit” is worth about 3 marks in almost every question. The recipe: DC sources to ground or open; each transistor becomes its model; drop anything with no signal on it; then KCL at the output. Only the ID equation is printed, so know gm = 2ID/VOV and gds = λID or be able to differentiate.

Intrinsic gain

The one ideaLoad a transistor with an ideal current source and its gain is −gm/gds: how strongly the gate controls the current divided by how weakly the drain does. This ratio, the intrinsic gain (about 100 here), is the most one transistor can give.

The circuit is the one in the “before and after” figure of Lesson 7: an NMOS with an ideal current source I from VDD into its drain, and VIN + vin on its gate.

The gain in two lines

After the conversion only the transistor model is left at the output node. KCL there:

gmvin+gdsvout=0⟹voutvin=−gmgds

Why it’s big: a tug of war

The ideal current source pins ID. Now raise VGS a little. The square law wants more current, but the current can’t change. The only other thing in the equation that can restore the balance is the (1 + λVDS) factor, so VDS has to fall. And because ID depends strongly on VGS but only weakly on VDS, VDS has to fall by a lot. A small input change forces a big output change: that’s gain.

In triode, ID also depends strongly on VDS, so VDS only needs to move a little and there’s hardly any gain. That is why amplifying transistors are kept in saturation.

  2. gds=λID=0.1×100=10μA/V.
  3. Gain =−1000/10=−100. In decibels: 20log10⁡100=40dB.

A shortcut worth knowing

Divide the two formulas and the current cancels:

gmgds=2ID/VOVλID=2λVOV

With λ around 0.04–0.1 V⁻¹ and an overdrive of a few hundred mV this is always much bigger than 1. So in saturation you can always assume gm ≫ gds, which almost every exam question lets you use.

Less current, more gain?

gm=2Kn′(W/L)ID grows like √ID, but gds=λID grows like ID. So the gain goes as 1/√ID: less current gives more gain. Infinite gain at zero current? No. As the current falls, the overdrive falls, and below about 100 mV the transistor enters weak inversion, where gm = ID/(n·kT/q) is proportional to ID. The gain then flattens at 1/(λ·n·kT/q), whatever the current.

The real price of current is speed. Currents charge capacitors (I = C dV/dt), so bandwidth scales with current. Doubling the current (in strong inversion) cuts the gain by √2 but doubles the bandwidth. The true trade-off is gain and bandwidth against power, which comes later in the course.

Real current sources aren’t ideal

If the load current source has its own output resistance rout, that adds a conductance at the output: gain = −gm/(gds + 1/rout), which is smaller. A lot of the rest of the course is about building loads that act as much like ideal current sources as possible.

A note for the labs

The AC analysis in Spectre is linear: it doesn’t know the supply is only 3 V. Apply the default 1 V AC input and you will “see” 100 V at the output. That is fine: it is just reading off the gain. Always check the DC operating point first (is everything saturated?).

Common-source stage: resistor load

The one ideaThe transistor turns an input voltage wiggle into a current wiggle (×gm); the resistor turns it back into a larger output voltage wiggle (×RL). Gain ≈ −gmRL. The catch: the DC current also flows through RL, and the DC voltage it drops limits how big RL can be.

Three ways to use one transistor

  • Common source (CS): in on the gate, out on the drain. The gain stage. High output resistance, because the drain current barely depends on the drain voltage.
  • Common drain, or source follower (CD): in on the gate, out on the source. A buffer: the output sits one VGS below the input (a DC level shift) and has low output resistance, because moving the output changes VGS, which strongly changes the current. Section 10.
  • Common gate (CG): gate at a DC bias, in on the source, out on the drain. Low input resistance; used in RF for impedance matching. Section 11.

Large-signal behaviour: sweep the input

  1. Vin below Vt: M1 off, no current, Vout = VDD.
  2. Vin just above Vt: M1 turns on in saturation (VDS is still large). Vout = VDD − IDRL falls, faster and faster because of the square law.
  3. At Vin = Vx, Vout has fallen to Vin − Vt: M1 enters triode and the gain collapses.
  4. Higher still: deep triode. M1 acts as the resistor RDS and Vout is set by the divider between RDS and RL.

The slope (the gain) grows as Vin rises, because more current means a larger gm. So the gain changes with the signal itself, which distorts it: this stage is non-linear. The most gain is just before M1 leaves saturation. You can see all of this in the amplifier lab (resistor preset).

Small-signal gain

KCL at the output:

gmvin+gdsvout+voutRL=0⟹voutvin=−gmgds+1/RL≈−gmRL

The approximation holds because RL is usually much smaller than rds, so RL dominates. The large-signal derivative (slides 110–111) gives the same answer. The lecturer recommends always using KCL: summing currents at the output stays easy as circuits grow.

  4. Gain =−400/(2+50)=−7.7, close to −gmRL=−8: about 18 dB.

Why not use RL = 500 kΩ to match rds? 100 µA × 500 kΩ = 50 V. The DC drop across the resistor caps the gain.

What limits this stage

  • Headroom. Gain ≈ gmRL, but IDRL can’t exceed VDD − VOV. With a 3 V supply instead of 5 V (same 100 µA), RL ≤ 25 kΩ and the gain drops to about 8.9. A realistic best is around ×10 (20 dB).
  • Process spread. Resistors can vary by ±20% and K′ by ±20% (so gm by about ±10%): the gain changes from chip to chip.
  • Non-linearity. gm changes as the signal changes the current.
  • No easy way out. A bigger RL, or a bigger W/L to get more gm (and more current), both push M1 out of saturation.

The bleed-current trick

M1 still carries 100 µA, so gm is unchanged, but only 50 µA flows in RL. For the same 4.5 V drop, RL can double to 90 kΩ. The gain rises from 14.7 to 24.8. It doesn’t quite double because rds = 200 kΩ is no longer much bigger than RL. The price is an extra transistor to make the bleed current. The Winter 2023 exam (P3) is built on this idea.

Common-source stage: diode-connected load

The one ideaSwap the resistor for a diode-connected transistor, which behaves like a resistor of value 1/gm. The gain becomes a ratio of two transconductances, gm1/gm2, so it stays constant (linear) and depends much less on process spread.

A diode-connected transistor is a 1/gm resistor

Tie gate to drain and the transistor is always saturated (Lesson 4). In the small-signal model, vgs is the very voltage v across the device, so the current is i = gmv: a resistor of 1/gm, in parallel with 1/gds, which is much larger and can be ignored. This is a low resistance (a few kΩ).

a transistor forced to agree with itself. Push the voltage up and it immediately conducts more to pull back, so it looks stiff: a small resistance.

Use it everywhere: whenever you see a diode-connected transistor in a small-signal circuit, replace it with 1/gm. Later in the course the same fact tells you which nodes are low-impedance, which matters for poles and bandwidth.

Large-signal behaviour (slides 117–120)

  • Vin = 0: M1 off. The output charges up through the PMOS diode, but only until VSG2 falls to |Vtp|; then M2 switches off too. So Vout stops at VDD − |Vtp|, not VDD.
  • Vin above Vtn: current flows, both transistors obey the square law, and substituting one into the other gives a straight line:
Vout=VDD−|Vtp|−Kn′(W/L)1Kp′(W/L)2(Vin−Vtn)

A straight line means a constant gain that doesn’t depend on the bias: the stage is linear. The reason: M1 converts voltage to current with a square law, and M2 converts current back to voltage with a square root. They cancel. Eventually M1 enters triode and the gain collapses, as before.

Small-signal gain

KCL at the output: gm1vin+(gm2+gds1+gds2)vout=0, so

Remember · diode-connected load voutvin=−gm1gm2+gds1+gds2≈−gm1gm2=−Kn′(W/L)1Kp′(W/L)2=−|VGS2|−|Vtp|VGS1−Vtn

The last form uses gm = 2I/VOV with the same current in both. For gain: a small overdrive on the driver and a large overdrive on the load.

Worked example · slides 122–125

VGS1 = 1 V, |VGS2| = 2.8 V, |Vt| = 0.8 V for both.

Gain = −(2.8 − 0.8)/(1 − 0.8) = −2.0/0.2 = −10, which is 20 dB.

Limits: |VGS2| = 2.8 V uses most of a 5 V supply, and the driver’s overdrive can’t go much below 0.2 V without leaving strong inversion. So once again the gain is about 10 and headroom sets the limit.

How steady is that gain? (recap, 25 Sept)

  • Over process: the gain depends on √(K′n/K′p) and a W/L ratio. K′ contains the mobility, which depends on doping and varies from wafer to wafer and batch to batch, roughly ±10%. NMOS and PMOS are doped in separate steps, so their K′ values are not correlated: one can come out 10% high while the other is 10% low. Make a million of these chips and the gain spreads by about ±10%.
  • Over temperature: mobility changes far more with temperature than with process, but that hardly matters here. The two transistors sit side by side on the same die at the same temperature, so both mobilities move together and their ratio holds.

So the diode load is linear and fairly predictable. The next two ideas (slide 126 and the folded load) remove even the K′n/K′p spread.

Show solution

  2. KCL: gm1vin + vout(gds1 + gm2 + gmb2 + gds2) = 0, so voutvin≈−gm1gm2+gmb2=−gm1gm2(1+η).

The fix: a folded load

Supply the output from a PMOS current source M3 and move the diode-connected NMOS M2 so its source is at ground (right-hand figure). No body effect. Because M3’s current is fixed, any change in M1’s current has to come out of M2, and M2 turns it into a voltage:

voutvin=−gm1gm2+gds1+gds2+gds3≈−gm1gm2=−(W/L)1ID1(W/L)2ID2

Why the current has to go into M2 (25 Sept): raise vin and M1 takes a little more of M3’s fixed current, so M2 gets a little less, and the diode turns that change into a voltage. This only works if M3’s own resistance, 1/gds3, is far higher than M2’s 1/gm2, so that almost all of the change goes into M2. A PMOS in saturation manages that easily.

The price is extra current. With the ordinary diode load, M1’s current is reused by the load; here M3 must feed both branches, so the stage is less power-efficient. The bonus is that you choose the split. From 100 µA you could run 50/50, or 80 µA in M1 and 20 µA in M2. Because gm ∝ √I, the 80/20 split doubles the gain compared with 50/50 (√(80/20) = 2), for the same transistor sizes. Putting a load between the output and ground like this (“folding” it) comes back later in the course, for example to save headroom in folded cascodes. Past papers P5 and P6 use exactly this circuit.

Common-source stage: current-source load

The one ideaA transistor in saturation acts like a current source with a large output resistance, yet it needs only a few hundred millivolts across it. Use a PMOS in saturation as the load and the gain jumps to gm1/(gds1 + gds2): typically 50 to 100. The catch: the DC bias becomes very touchy.

Why a current-source load

With a resistor or a diode, more gain costs DC voltage: a bigger RL or a smaller gm2 both mean a bigger drop. A PMOS biased in saturation only needs VSD ≥ its overdrive (roughly 0.2–0.4 V), but its small-signal resistance is rds2 = 1/gds2, hundreds of kΩ. Gain and headroom are decoupled.

Picture the PMOS I–V curve in saturation: almost flat. A tiny change in current needs a huge change in voltage, which is exactly what a high resistance means.

Large-signal behaviour (slides 129–130)

  1. Low Vin: M1 off; M2 pulls the output to VDD, so M2 has no voltage across it and is in triode.
  2. Vin above Vtn: M1 on in saturation, M2 still in triode; the output starts to fall.
  3. Both saturated: the output drops very steeply. This narrow window is the high-gain region.
  4. Higher Vin: M1 enters triode and the gain is gone.

Useful output swing: VDD − VSAT1 − VSAT2. The input window for high gain is tiny (tens of millivolts), so biasing this stage without feedback is hard. Slide 130 gets the gain the long way, by writing both square-law equations with λ, solving for Vout and differentiating; the answer is the same as the small-signal one below. Try it in the amplifier lab (current-source preset).

The signal-path insight

M2’s gate is at a DC voltage (VBIAS2) and its source is at VDD. Neither can wiggle, so vgs2 = 0 and the current source gm2vgs2 carries nothing: it is an open circuit. All that M2 contributes is its output resistance 1/gds2. The lecturer’s rule: follow the signal. A transistor with no signal on its gate or source is just its output resistance.

The gain

Part (iii): why doubling W2 alone breaks the stage (slides 136–138)

With VBIAS2 unchanged, a twice-as-wide M2 wants to pass twice the current, 256 µA. But M1’s current is pinned at 128 µA by VBIAS1. Two current sources in series that disagree cannot both get their way.

  1. The only thing free to move is the output voltage. It rises: that gives M1 a bit more VDS (slightly more current) and M2 a bit less VSD (slightly less current).
  2. But in saturation the current hardly depends on VDS. Per volt of output rise, M1 gains only about 5 µA and M2 loses about 10 µA (slide 136 uses a round 4 µA/V), nowhere near the 128 µA mismatch.
  3. So the output keeps rising. Once it passes 2.6 V (VSD2 below M2’s 0.4 V overdrive), M2 is in triode and its current falls. The output settles at about 2.87 V, only 0.13 V below VDD, where the weakened M2 finally matches M1. The gain has collapsed.

a tug of war between two teams that each pull with a fixed force. If the forces are exactly equal, the rope (the output) can rest in the middle. If one team is even slightly stronger, the rope slides all the way to its side. Doubling W2 makes the PMOS team twice as strong, so the output slides up until M2 runs out of room (triode) and weakens, just below VDD.

The fix (slide 137): make M1 carry the doubled current too. Current ∝ VOV², so M1’s overdrive must rise by √2: 0.4 × √2 = 0.566 V, giving VBIAS1 = 0.8 + 0.566 ≈ 1.366 V (the slide rounds to 1.365 V). Both transistors are saturated again, now at 256 µA. What happens to the gain? gm1 = √(2K′n(W/L)I) rises only by √2, while gds = λI doubles, so the gain falls by √2: 62.5/√2 = −44 (32.9 dB). The high-gain window has moved to a higher input voltage (slide 138). The other way out, which the lecturer also mentioned, is to double W1 as well: M1 then carries 256 µA at the same 0.4 V overdrive, so the gain stays at −62.5, at twice the power.

The CMOS inverter as an amplifier

The one ideaDrive the PMOS gate with the input too. Now both transistors turn the input into signal current, and both currents push the output the same way, so the gain doubles to (gm1 + gm2)/(gds1 + gds2) for the same current and overdrive (as on slide 139). It is simply the digital inverter, biased half-way.

How it amplifies

Bias the input half-way so both transistors are on and saturated (the analogue inverter from Lesson 1). Then nudge the input up a little:

  1. M1’s VGS rises, so M1 wants more current.
  2. M2’s VSG falls, so M2 wants to give less current.
  3. They are in series and must carry the same current, so the output moves to settle the argument. It falls, which gives M2 more VSD and M1 less VDS.
  4. In saturation the current depends strongly on VGS but only weakly on VDS, so the output has to move much further than the input did. That ratio is the gain.

Compare Lesson 11: there M2 had a fixed gate and just sat there as a high resistance. Here M2 actively helps: when M1 pulls harder, M2 lets go.

Where it is used: a clock buffer

The lecturer’s example comes from radio chips. An RF transceiver mixes a signal of several GHz down to baseband, and the mixer needs a clean clock. That clock often comes from an off-chip crystal oscillator and arrives through a coupling capacitor as a small sine wave, at a DC level you don’t control. A chain of inverter amplifiers gains it up until it becomes a square wave from 0 to VDD. The trick is biasing each stage inside its narrow high-gain window.

  1. The capacitor blocks the unknown DC level of the incoming clock, so the chip can set its own.
  2. Stage 1 biases itself. Its input is tied to its output, so M1 and M2 are both diode-connected. The node settles at the inverter’s switching point, about VDD/2 when the two have equal drive strength, with both saturated.
  3. Stage 2 is a copy. M3 matches M1 and M4 matches M2 (same W/L). Its gates sit at stage 1’s voltage, so it carries the same currents at the same VGS, and its output settles at that same voltage. It is biased in its high-gain region by construction, not by luck.
  4. A few more copies turn the millivolt-level sine into a full-swing square wave.

a replica. Instead of calculating the right bias voltage and hoping it is accurate, you build a twin of the stage, let the twin find its own balance point, and hand that voltage to the real stage.

The resistor version: bias and gain from stage 1

With a plain wire, stage 1 only sets the bias: its output equals its input, so it adds no gain. Replace the wire with a large resistor R. No DC current flows through R (the input is capacitor-coupled and gates draw no current), so both ends of R sit at the same DC voltage and the bias is unchanged. But the signal now sees a stage with gain:

Remember · inverter with a feedback resistor voutvin=−gm1+gm2−1/Rgds1+gds2+1/R
Show the derivation (the lecturer suggested it as practice)

Same model as above, plus R from vin to vout, which carries (vout − vin)/R out of the output node. KCL at the output:

(gm1+gm2)vin+(gds1+gds2)vout+vout−vinR=0

Collect the vout terms: vout(gds1 + gds2 + 1/R) = −vin(gm1 + gm2 − 1/R), which is the result. The −1/R on top is signal leaking forward through R.

Sanity checks: R → ∞ gives back −(gm1 + gm2)/(gds1 + gds2). R → 0 gives (1/R)/(1/R) = +1: the output simply equals the input, which is the plain-wire case.

Numbers (my illustration, slide 139 values): R = 1 MΩ gives −(800 − 1)/(10 + 1) ≈ −73 (37 dB); R = 100 kΩ gives −(800 − 10)/(10 + 10) ≈ −40 (32 dB).

Why bother? Every stage in the chain draws current while the signal is switching, so getting gain out of the biasing stage means fewer stages and less power.

In the clock buffer, why does no DC current flow through R?

One end of R connects only to transistor gates and to a coupling capacitor, and neither passes DC current. There is nowhere for a DC current in R to go, so both ends of R sit at the same DC voltage.

Why does stage 2 of the clock buffer land in its high-gain region without any tuning?

It is an identical copy of stage 1 with the same gate voltage, so its transistors want the same currents at the same VGS. The only output voltage that balances them is the same voltage as stage 1’s, which is the switching point where both are saturated.

The one ideaEvery stage so far does the same two things: it turns the input voltage into a signal current (the stage’s transconductance Gm), and that current flows into all the conductances at the output node. PMOS driver with a feedback resistor

Lessons 6, 12, 13 · 10 marks

All transistors saturated; gm1, gm2, 1/RF ≫ gds1, gds2. VDD = 3 V, ID2 = 200 µA, Vtn = 0.75 V, Vtp = −0.75 V, K′n = 200 µA/V², K′p = 50 µA/V², CF = 1 pF, CL = 9 pF. CIN is a large DC-blocking capacitor (a short for signals).

  1. Draw the small-signal equivalent circuit. [3]
  2. Calculate RBIAS to bias M2 with a drain current of 200 µA. [2]
  3. Calculate gm1, the transconductance of M1. [2]
  4. What value of RF gives a low-frequency gain from vin to vout of 16.9 dB? [3]

Part (ii), the high-frequency transfer function, and parts (vi)–(vii), break frequencies and a Bode plot, need Section 18 onwards.

Show solution
  1. CIN is a short, so M1’s gate carries vin. RF in parallel with CF connects the gate (vin) to the output. At the output: gm1vin (M1, from the output to its source at AC ground), 1/gds1, 1/gds2 and CL. M2’s gate sits at a DC mirror voltage, so gm2vgs2 = 0 and M2 is just 1/gds2.
  2. M2 (32/1) shares its VGS with M3 (3.2/1), so I3 = 200/10 = 20 µA. Then 20 = 100 × 3.2 × VOV², so VOV = 0.25 V and VGS3 = 1.0 V. RBIAS = (3 − 1.0) V / 20 µA = 100 kΩ.
  3. M1 carries the same 200 µA: gm1 = √(2 × 50 × 32 × 200) µA/V = 800 µA/V, with an overdrive of 0.5 V.
  4. At low frequency the capacitors are open. KCL at the output is Lesson 12’s feedback-resistor equation with only one transconductance: gm1vin + (gds1 + gds2)vout + (vout − vin)/RF = 0, so voutvin=−gm1−1/RFgds1+gds2+1/RF With 1/RF ≫ gds this is −(gm1 − 1/RF)RF = −(gm1RF − 1). 16.9 dB is a gain of 1016.9/20 = 7.0, so gm1RF = 8 and RF = 8/(800 µA/V) = 10 kΩ. Dropping the −1 would give 8.75 kΩ; the tidy 10 kΩ suggests the examiner intended the full expression.

Bonus insight: no DC current flows in RF, so M1’s gate sits at the output’s DC voltage and M1 behaves like a diode at DC. With 200 µA its VSG = 0.75 + 0.5 = 1.25 V, so the output settles at 3 − 1.25 = 1.75 V by itself. M2 then has 1.75 V across it against a 0.25 V overdrive, so it is saturated too. This is the self-biasing trick from the clock buffer.

Which region?

NMOS: on if VGS > Vt; saturated if VGD ≤ Vt (i.e. VDS ≥ VGS − Vt).

PMOS: on if VSG > |Vtp|; saturated if VDG ≤ |Vtp|.

Gate tied to drain ⇒ always saturated (if on).

Drain current

ID=Kn′2WLVOV2(1+λVDS) IDtriode=Kn′WL(VOVVDS−VDS22)

Deep triode: RDS = 1/(K′ W/L VOV). VOV = VGS − Vt.

Biasing

VOV=2IDKn′W/L

λ = 0 for DC. Same current and W/L ⇒ same VGS. Same VGS ⇒ current ∝ W/L. Middle of a stack = upper gate − VGS. λ = 0.04/L (L in µm).

Small-signal

gm=Kn′WLVOV=2Kn′WLID=2IDVOV

gds = λID, rds = 1/gds, gmb = ηgm. Diode-connected = 1/gm. DC voltage → ground; DC current → open. Same model for PMOS.

CS gains

Gain = −(signal current per volt)/(sum of conductances at the output).

Ideal I: −gm1/gds1 · Resistor: ≈ −gm1RL · Diode: ≈ −gm1/gm2 · Current source: −gm1/(gds1 + gds2) · Inverter: −(gm1 + gm2)/(gds1 + gds2).

Intrinsic gain gm/gds = 2/(λVOV).

Two-port shortcut

av=−GmGout+GL

Gm: short the output, take iout/vin. Gout: short the input, take i/v at the output (looking down + looking up). Then add the load’s GL.

Inverter: Gm = gm1 + gm2. Feedback R from input to output: −(Gm − 1/R)/(Gout + 1/R).

Numbers

K′n = 200 µA/V², K′p = 50 µA/V² (course values). Vt ≈ 0.7–0.8 V. Keep VOV ≥ 0.2 V. kT/q = 26 mV at room temperature.

dB = 20 log10|A|: ×2 = 6 dB, ×10 = 20 dB, ×100 = 40 dB.
