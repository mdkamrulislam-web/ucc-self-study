Lesson 1Notes 1

What optimisation is

The one ideaAn optimisation problem has three parts: the decisions you control, the rules those decisions must obey, and one number to make as large or as small as possible. This module is mostly about writing real problems in that shape; software then finds the best answer.

The three parts, in a timetable

The lecturer’s first example is a department timetable: every lecture needs a room and a time.

PartWhat it isIn the timetable
Decision variablesWhat you chooseThe room and time slot for each lecture
ConstraintsRules every acceptable answer must obeyNo two lectures in one room at once; nobody in two rooms at once; every lecture happens somewhere; Dr Smith can’t lecture on Tuesday mornings
Objective functionThe single number you optimiseMinimise the lectures on Monday morning, or minimise the variance in lectures per day

A timetable that breaks a constraint is useless however good its objective looks. So the constraints decide which answers are allowed (the feasible ones) and the objective ranks them.

The same shape fits factory production lines, circuit-board layout, aircraft crew rostering, scheduling TV adverts, choosing which parts of a forest to harvest and managing stock. The objective might be cost or risk to minimise, or profit or the chance of success to maximise.

Where it came from

The original optimisation field is Operational Research (the UK name) or Operations Research (the US name, now more common); both are shortened to OR. It is a set of techniques for making decisions under conflicting goals, limited resources, uncertainty and deadlines.

The story in one minute (not examined)
  • 1936: the RAF saw that radar could transform air defence, but the mass of radar data wasn’t being used well. A team studied how the system was operated, as opposed to the technology, and the term OR was coined.
  • 1939: the last pre-war air-defence exercise (33,000 men, 1,300 aircraft, 110 anti-aircraft guns, 700 searchlights, 100 barrage balloons) went so well that OR teams were set up across RAF commands.
  • WWII: OR advised sending no fighter squadrons to France (they defended Britain instead), reorganised flying and maintenance for a 61% improvement, and raised the chance of sinking a U-boat from 2–3% to 40% by studying depth-charge settings, lethal radius and aiming errors.
  • After the war: in the UK it stayed mainly in iron, steel and coal; in the US it moved into universities, and the RAND Corporation (a 1950s think tank) invented many OR methods.
  • The “programming” in linear programming is the old sense of the word, meaning planning. It has nothing to do with writing code.

Anatomy of a linear program

The one ideaA linear program (LP) chooses values for the decision variables to maximise or minimise a linear objective, subject to linear constraints. Linear means every term is a number times one variable: no variables multiplied together, no powers, no dividing by a variable.

Remember · the general form (Notes 1) maximise or minimiseZ=∑j=1ncjxj subject to∑j=1naijxj{≤,=,≥}bi(i=1,…,m) xj≤uj,xj≥0(j=1,…,n)

You choose the decision variables xj. The parameters aij,bi,cj,uj are given: cj is the profit or cost per unit, aij how much of resource i one unit of xj uses, bi how much of resource i there is, and uj an upper bound (a market limit, say). The non-negativity restrictions xj≥0 are usual but optional: a variable without one is called unrestricted or free.

A recipe for modelling

  1. Variables. What does the decision-maker choose? Name each one with its unit: “P = units of product P made per week”.
  2. Objective. What is maximised or minimised? Write it as (value per unit × variable), summed.
  3. Constraints. Read the problem one sentence at a time. Each limit becomes one inequality or equation. Give each a name.
  4. Bounds and signs. Upper bounds and ≥0.
  5. Solve, then interpret. Put the numbers back into the story (Lesson 4).

Lesson 3Notes 1–2

Solving a two-variable LP by graph

The one ideaWith two variables, each constraint is a straight line that splits the plane in two. The points on the allowed side of every line form the feasible region, a polygon. The best point is always at a corner (a vertex), so you only need to check the corners.

The lecturer’s example: a furniture maker earns $6 per table and $8 per chair. A table takes 30 board feet of wood and 5 hours of labour; a chair takes 20 board feet and 10 hours. There are 300 board feet and 110 hours available. With T tables and C chairs:

maximise Z=6T+8Csubject to30T+20C≤300,5T+10C≤110,T,C≥0

The method, step by step

  1. Draw the axes, one variable across and one up. With both variables ≥0 only the top-right quarter matters.
  2. Draw each boundary line. Replace ≤ by = and find where the line meets the axes. 30T+20C=300 meets the T axis at T=10 (put C=0) and the C axis at C=15 (put T=0). Labour: T=22 and C=11.
  3. Pick the allowed side. Test a point that is not on the line, usually the origin: 30(0)+20(0)=0≤300 is true, so the origin’s side is allowed.
  4. Shade the feasible region, where every allowed side overlaps.
  5. Find the corners. Each corner is where two boundary lines cross, so solve those two equations together.
  6. Evaluate Z at every corner. The best one is the optimum.
Worked example · the top corner

The corner where the two lines cross solves 30T+20C=300and5T+10C=110. Double the second: 10T+20C=220. Subtract it from the first: 20T=80, so T=4. Then 5(4)+10C=110 gives C=9.

Why a corner? Slide the profit line

Every point on the line 6T+8C=k earns the same profit k. Changing k slides that line without turning it: all these iso-profit lines are parallel. Push the line in the direction that raises profit until it is about to leave the region. The last point it touches is a corner, or a whole edge if the line is parallel to that edge (Lesson 5).

Exam angle

Winter 2025 Q1 (15 marks) is this method on this very example with new names: paintings and sculptures, €600 and €800. Show the model, axes with scales, each line labelled, the region shaded, every corner with its Z value, and the answer in words. See P1.

Check yourself
The origin lies on the line 2x−y=0. How do you find the allowed side of 2x−y≤0?

Test a point off the line, such as (1,0): 2−0=2≤0 is false, so that side is excluded. The allowed side is above the line, where y≥2x.

If tables earned $6 and chairs $12, where is the optimum?

6T+12C is parallel to the labour line 5T+10C=110 (both are multiples of T+2C). Z=132 at (0,11) and at (4,9), and at every point on the edge between them. Try it: open the LP lab and change the objective.

Why can’t the optimum be strictly inside the region?

From an inside point you can take a small step in the direction that raises Z and still be feasible, so that point wasn’t the best.

Lesson 4Notes 2

Reading a solution: tight, slack and redundant

The one ideaPut the optimum back into every constraint. Constraints that hold with equality are tight (also called active or binding): they are the bottlenecks. Constraints with room to spare have slack, and loosening them gains nothing. Constraints that never touch the feasible region are redundant and can be deleted.

The factory, interpreted

Plug P=81.82, Q=16.36, R=60 back into the factory model from Lesson 2:

ConstraintLimitUsedStatus
Machine A24002400tight
Machine B24002400tight
Machine C24002285slack 115 min
Machine D24001064slack 1336 min
Market P10081.82slack
Market Q4016.36slack
Market R6060tight

So the bottlenecks are machines A and B and the market for R. More time on A or B, or a bigger market for R, might raise profit. More time on C or D, or bigger markets for P and Q, would change nothing: machines C and D already sit idle part of the week.

Remember · slack and surplus slack=bi−∑jaijxj(for ≤),surplus=∑jaijxj−bi(for ≥)

A constraint is tight when its slack or surplus is 0. In lp_solve, the block “Actual values of the constraints” lists each named constraint’s left-hand side at the optimum: compare it with the right-hand side.

Redundant constraints

Fix R=60 (its value at the optimum) and the factory becomes a two-variable problem you can draw. Each machine loses R’s share: machine A has 2400−10(60)=1800 minutes left for P and Q, and so on.

maximise45P+60Qsubject to20P+10Q≤1800(A),12P+28Q≤1440(B)15P+6Q≤1440(C),10P+15Q≤2400(D),P≤100,Q≤40

C, D and P≤100 are redundant here: delete them and the region is unchanged. (They weren’t redundant in the three-product model; fixing R made them so.) In general you can’t tell by looking, but if you know a constraint is redundant, delete it. In Lab 1, “no more than 9 apples” is redundant, because the banana rules already stop you packing more than 8.

The abstractions behind the model

  • Fractions. You can’t make 81.82 units. Rounding is reasonable here, but in general it can give a poor answer or break a constraint. Integer programming (Lesson 9) fixes this.
  • Linearity. Real costs often aren’t linear: buying more material can get cheaper per unit.
  • Certainty. Market demand is really an estimate, not a known number.
  • Everything else. The model assumes machines A and B can actually be scheduled to produce this mix, which might not be true. Part of the art of modelling is knowing what can be left out.
Going further: dual values (not in the notes)

Run lp_solve with -S4 and it also prints a dual value for each constraint: how much the objective improves per extra unit of that constraint’s right-hand side, valid over the from–till range printed beside it. In the wood example one more board foot is worth $0.10 and one more labour hour $0.60. A slack constraint always has dual value 0. The notes call this sensitivity analysis and skip it.

Exam angle

“Which constraints are redundant?” was part of Lab 1. “Interpret the solution” wants the tight constraints named as bottlenecks, in the problem’s own words (“machine B is fully used”).

Check yourself
At the wood optimum (4,9), which constraints are tight?

Both: wood 30(4)+20(9)=300 and labour 5(4)+10(9)=110.

A constraint touches the feasible region at a single corner. Is it redundant?

Possibly. Test it by deleting it: if the region doesn’t change, it is redundant. In Lab 1, a≥0 touches only at (0,0) and is redundant, because b≤2a and b≥0.5a together already force a≥0.

Lesson 5Notes 2

When things go wrong: the special cases

The one ideaNot every LP has one neat answer. There may be infinitely many optimal points, a feasible region that runs off to infinity, an objective that improves forever, or no feasible point at all. The last two nearly always mean a mistake in the model.

Heads-up · the unbounded example in the notes

The notes print this case with −X1+4X2≤10 (not ≥). As printed, the region is bounded (X1≤4 and so X2≤3.5), and lp_solve finds Z=2.5 at (0,2.5). The notes’ picture shows a region open upwards, which needs the ≥ version drawn above. Its sentence “X2 has an upper bound but X1 doesn’t” also reads the wrong way round for that picture: X1 is capped at 4 and X2 is free to grow. The rule behind it holds: if every variable has an upper bound, the optimum is always finite.

The cases side by side

One more version of infeasible: the constraints agree with each other, but only at negative values, and x≥0 rules those out. Real problems are normally feasible and bounded by their resource limits, so the last three cases usually mean an error by the modeller: an omitted constraint, a typo. With more than two variables you can’t draw the region, so these mistakes are harder to spot, but optima are still at corners (“extreme points”) when they exist.

lp_solve quirk

If a variable that improves the objective appears in no constraint at all, lp_solve 5.5 doesn’t report “unbounded”. It sets that variable to its “infinite” bound, 1e+30, and prints an absurd objective such as 9e+30. Treat any value near 1e+30 as “unbounded”, and look for the variable you forgot to constrain.

Exam angle

If asked what can happen when solving an LP, list the cases with a small sketch each: multiple optima (harmless), unbounded (a missing constraint), infeasible (conflicting constraints).

Check yourself
Your model of a real factory comes back unbounded. What is the likeliest cause?

A missing constraint (a resource or market limit you forgot), or a ≥ typed where you meant ≤.

Can an LP with an unbounded feasible region still have a single optimum?

Yes, as in the second picture above. An unbounded region is not the same as an unbounded objective.

lp_solve and the LP file format

The one idealp_solve reads a plain text file: the objective first, then the constraints, then any int/bin/free declarations, every statement ending in a semicolon. The format has a handful of traps, and each one silently changes your model instead of stopping with an error.

lp_solve is free, open source and has no limit on model size. It solves LPs and mixed-integer programs, and the labs are marked on its print-outs. It runs on the lab machines; on your own computer you type lp_solve filename.lp in a terminal (the notes warn that the Windows version can be fiddly, and suggest Linux). The LP lab below reads the same format, so you can check a file anywhere.

The traps, all tested on lp_solve 5.5.2

1 · A missing colon turns a name into a variable

A real Lab 1 file contained these lines:

Written

max: apples + bananas;
max_bananas: bananas <= 4;
max_apples: apples <= 9;
upper_bound_bananas <= 2 apples;
lower_bound_bananas >= 0.5 apples;

How lp_solve reads the last two

R3: -2 apples +upper_bound_bananas <= 0;
R4: -0.5 apples +lower_bound_bananas >= 0;

/* two new variables; bananas is
   no longer tied to apples */

With bananas freed from the apples, lp_solve reports Z=13 at 9 apples and 4 bananas, a point that breaks the problem’s own rule (9 apples need at least 4.5 bananas). With the colons, and bananas written into each line, the answer is Z=12 at 8 apples and 4 bananas:

upper_bound_bananas: bananas <= 2 apples;
lower_bound_bananas: bananas >= 0.5 apples;

2 · 2e1 is the number 20

Numbers can be written in scientific notation, so 3d1 - 2e1 <= 16 is read as 3d1−20≤16: the variable e1 vanishes, and because only one variable is left, the line becomes the bound d1≤12. Put a space in: 2 e1. (The notes’ own example.)

3 · Side-by-side terms are added

3 x y <= 16 means 3x+y≤16, never 3xy. Likewise 3 x 2 y is 3x+2y.

4 · < is <=, even for integers

With int x;, the line x > 3 still allows x=3. Write x >= 4. (The lecturer calls this “a terrible feature for integer variables; but there it is”.)

5 · One variable and no name makes a bound, not a constraint

x1 >= 1; sets a lower bound on x1. It won’t appear under “Actual values of the constraints”, and the notes advise not naming bounds. But bounds have a side effect:

bin throws away earlier bounds

bin r; sets r’s bounds to 0 and 1, replacing any bound set earlier. Type the umbrella puzzle from Notes 6 straight into a file, with c = 1; and r = 1; followed by bin r, u, j, c;, and lp_solve prints “lower bound on variable redefined” and solves with every variable 0. Give those lines names so they are constraints: raining: r = 1; and incafe: c = 1;. Then the answer is the one in the notes: c=r=j=1, u=0.

6 · Two spellings lp_solve rejects

=< and => are syntax errors. So is a constraint written after an int or bin declaration: declarations must come last.

Exam angle

The exam doesn’t test file syntax, but the labs are marked on lp_solve print-outs, and each of these traps gives a confident, wrong answer. Always check that the output’s variable list contains only the variables you meant.

Check yourself
What does lp_solve make of 3 x1 x2 >= 2;?

The constraint 3x1+x2≥2. It has two variables, so it is a constraint, not a bound; with no name it is called R1, R2, … after its position among the constraints.

What is the difference between x >= 1; and c1: x >= 1;?

The first is a bound on x; the second is a named constraint. The constraint appears in the output, and a later bin x can’t erase it.

Why is max: 2a + 3b; c1: a + b <= 4; a <= -2; infeasible?

a <= -2 is a bound, and a’s lower bound is still the default 0. No value lies between 0 and −2. To allow negative values, write a >= -1e30; (or declare free a; at the end).

Modelling patterns: blending, covering and flows

The one ideaMost LPs are variations on a few patterns. Blending chooses proportions that add up to 1. Covering makes sure every requirement is met by enough of the options that serve it. Network flow balances what goes into each point with what comes out.

Blending: animal feed

Mix limestone, corn and soybean meal into feed with 0.8–1.2% calcium, at least 22% protein and at most 5% fibre, as cheaply as possible.

The conservation constraint L+C+S=1 is what makes them proportions. The answer: about 3% limestone, 65% corn and 32% soybean, at about 49 cents per kilogram (lp_solve: 0.0282, 0.6486, 0.3233 and 49.16). Limestone is cheap, but it has no protein, so it can’t replace much soybean.

Network flow with losses: power distribution

How LPs are solved

The one ideaSolvers don’t test every point. The simplex method walks from corner to corner along the edges of the feasible region, improving the objective at each step, and stops when no neighbouring corner is better. Interior-point methods cut through the inside of the region instead.

SimplexInterior point
RouteAlong the boundary, corner to cornerThrough the inside of the region
Guaranteed speedNot known to be polynomialPolynomial in the problem size
In practiceUsually fastest; in almost every commercial packageBest on very large, sparse problems
History1940s, a landmark of OR, much improved sinceNewer

A problem is sparse when each constraint involves only a few of the variables, so most coefficients are 0.

Why not solve it like simultaneous equations?

At school you solve equations: every line must hold exactly. An LP has inequalities, and you don’t know in advance which ones will be tight at the optimum. A corner is exactly a choice of tight constraints (two of them, in two dimensions), so the simplex method keeps guessing a set of tight constraints, solves those equations (the part you learned at school), and checks whether a neighbouring guess is better. That is why large LPs, with thousands of variables, still solve quickly.

Exam angle

Expect at most a sentence or two: simplex moves between vertices of the feasible region; interior-point methods go through its inside and run in polynomial time. You won’t be asked to run simplex by hand.

Check yourself
Why does simplex only ever visit corners?

If an optimum exists, one is at a corner (Lesson 3), so the corners are the only candidates worth checking, and moving along an edge is how you get from one to the next.

Simplex reaches a corner where neither neighbour is better. Why can it stop?

The feasible region of an LP is convex (it has no dents), so a corner that beats its neighbours beats every point. There are no traps of “local best but not global best”, which will matter later for local search.

The travelling salesman problem

The one ideaThe TSP asks for the shortest tour that visits every city once and returns home. As an IP: a binary xij for each trip i→j, exactly one trip out of and one into every city, plus extra constraints that forbid small loops (subtours) that miss some cities.

Distances come as a matrix Dij. If Dij=Dji everywhere the problem is symmetric; one-way roads, road quality or flight prices make it asymmetric (ATSP). A tour is a Hamiltonian cycle on a complete graph, or equally an ordering of the cities, such as (2, 1, 4, 3): drive 2 → 1 → 4 → 3, then 3 → 2 to get home.

The 1954 model (Dantzig, Fulkerson and Johnson)

minimise Z=∑i∑jDijxijsubject to∑jxij=1(each i),∑ixij=1(each j)

One successor and one predecessor for every city (there is no xii). That isn’t enough: two separate loops, such as 1 → 2 → 1 and 3 → 4 → 3, also give every city one way in and one way out. So for each possible subtour, with edge set S:

∑(i,j)∈Sxij≤|S|−1

Not all the edges of a subtour can be used together. Only subtours of up to n/2 cities need listing, since a longer one leaves the rest in a shorter one. The catch: there are 2n−1−n−1 of them, far too many for big n to write out in advance.

A paper company sells rolls in five standard widths, 5, 8, 12, 15 and 17 feet, but its machine only makes 25-foot rolls. Orders must be cut from those. The demands are 40, 35, 30, 25 and 20 rolls of the five widths. Cut as few 25-foot rolls as possible.

Variables: xj = the number of 25-foot rolls cut with pattern j, a whole number. Minimise Z=x1+x2+⋯+x11, with one covering constraint per width:

x2+2x4+x6+2x7+x9+3x10+5x11≥40(5 ft)x1+x3+x6+3x8+2x9+x10≥35(8 ft)2x5+x6+x7≥30(12 ft)x3+x4≥25(15 ft)x1+x2≥20(17 ft)xj≥0 and integer

Read one row to see the pattern: pattern 5 (12 + 12) gives two 12-foot rolls, patterns 6 and 7 give one each, and no other pattern gives any, so the 12-foot row is 2x5+x6+x7≥30. It is the covering idea from the car-rental problem (Lesson 7), with the number of pieces per pattern as the coefficient.

How many rolls? · my working, checked with lp_solve

The notes list three different optimal plans; all use 64 rolls. Two quick checks show nothing better exists:

  • Total width ordered: 40(5)+35(8)+30(12)+25(15)+20(17)=1555 feet, and 1555/25=62.2, so at least 63 rolls.
  • Solving without the integer restriction (the LP relaxation) gives 63.33 rolls. The IP can’t beat its own relaxation, and the number of rolls is a whole number, so at least 64. A plan with 64 is therefore optimal.

lp_solve finds a fourth 64-roll plan: 20 of pattern 1, 9 of pattern 3, 16 of pattern 4, 15 of pattern 5, 2 of pattern 8 and 2 of pattern 11. The LP lab on this page finds yet another (16, 4, 7, 18, 15, 4 of patterns 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8). Several optimal plans is normal here; the total, 64, is what matters.

The same model fits timber and other industries. Cutting is closely related to packing (fitting shapes into a limited space, like Lab 1’s box of fruit) and to partitioning (splitting a set of numbers into groups to optimise something). The catch: with more widths the number of patterns explodes, and listing them all becomes impractical. Techniques such as column generation create useful patterns only as needed; the module doesn’t cover them.

A colour printing firm prints labels (cat-food flavours: beef, rabbit, tuna, chicken, mixed) from metal templates. Each template has s slots (s=9 here), each slot holding one label design, and each template is printed for some number of runs. Choose the templates and their runs so every flavour meets its demand, with as few runs as possible in total.

Tuna appears twice on template 1 and once on template 2, so this plan prints 15,000×2+23,000×1=53,000 tuna labels. (The notes print 43,000; the sum is 53,000.)

The natural model

Fix the number of templates (the model can’t easily choose it, because it would decide how many variables there are). Integer variables: ri = runs of template i; tij = slots on template i given to flavour j, from 0 to s. With demand Dj for flavour j:

minimise ∑irisubject to∑jtij=s(each i),tij≥0,∑iritij≥Dj(each j)

The last line multiplies two variables, ritij, so it isn’t linear (Lesson 2). Some solvers accept it; a pure IP solver like lp_solve doesn’t.

A linear version, and why it is weak

Model every slot explicitly. Keep ri, drop tij, and add binaries aijk=1 if flavour j is in slot k of template i, plus integers fijk for what that slot contributes to flavour j’s total:

∑jaijk=1(each slot),∑i∑kfijk≥Dj(each flavour) fijk≤ri+M(1−aijk),ri≤fijk+M(1−aijk),fijk≤Maijk

It is correct, but it has two drawbacks the lecturer points out:

  • Big-M constraints make the IP harder to solve (Lessons 11 and 13).
  • Symmetry. Shuffling the slots of a template gives a different set of aijk values for the same design, so each design has an enormous number of equivalent representations. The solver may explore them all. Extra symmetry-breaking constraints can help; the module doesn’t cover them.

Template design is genuinely hard: the optimal solutions to quite small instances are not known. It is the last IP example in the notes.

Exam angle

For a cutting or packing question: list the patterns, define “xj = number of times pattern j is used”, write one covering row per item (coefficient = pieces of that item in the pattern), minimise the total. If a model needs a product of two variables, say it is nonlinear and show how binaries plus big-M linearise it, and mention the cost (big-M, symmetry).

Check yourself
What does pattern 10 contain, and how much does it waste?

One 8-foot and three 5-foot rolls: 23 feet used, 2 feet of waste. That is why x10 has coefficient 3 in the 5-foot row and 1 in the 8-foot row.

Why leave out patterns that waste 5 feet or more?

Waste of 5 feet or more could have been cut into another 5-foot roll, so a pattern with that much waste is never better than the same pattern with an extra 5-foot piece.

Why is ritij≥Dj a problem for lp_solve?

It multiplies two variables, so it is nonlinear. LP and IP allow only a number times a single variable.

Show solution

The LP

max⁡/min⁡∑jcjxjs.t.∑jaijxj{≤,=,≥}bi,0≤xj≤uj
      
Linear: numbers × single variables. x/y≤k → x≤ky. Constants in the objective don’t change the optimum.

    

    
Graph method

      
  • Intercepts for each line; test the origin for the side.
  • Region = overlap of all allowed sides.
  • Corners = pairs of lines solved together.
  • Z at every corner; best wins (or slide the iso-line).

    

    
Reading a solution

      
Tight (active, binding): LHS = RHS, a bottleneck. Slack: room left, loosening gains nothing. Redundant: never touches the region; delete it.

      
Special cases: many optima (objective ∥ a constraint), unbounded (missing limit), infeasible (conflict). lp_solve prints ~1e+30 for a variable in no constraint.

    

    
lp_solve traps

      
  • name: expr; needs the colon.
  • 2e1 = 20; write 2 e1.
  • 3 x y = 3x + y.
  • < = <=, even for int.
  • Unnamed single-variable line = bound; bin resets bounds.
  • =< rejected; declarations last.

if x then yx≤y

          
not both / if x then not yx+y≤1

          
x iff yx=y

          
if x or y then wx+y≤2w

Big-M patterns

      
    
        
  • Fixed cost: x≤My, add cost × y.
    • 
        
  • Switch off a rule: LHS≤b+MY or LHS≥b−MY; ∑Y≤ allowed breaks.
    • 
        
  • Either/or: r≤100+M(1−b), r≥100−Mb.
    • 
        
  • From a list: x=∑vkyk, ∑yk=1.
    •

∑bi=1; libi≤xi≤uibi; x=∑xi; y=∑(sixi+cibi).

    

    
Cutting and packing

      
xj = uses of pattern j (integer); min ∑xj; one row per width: ∑j(pieces of that width in j)xj≥ demand. Bounds: total width ÷ roll width, and the LP relaxation rounded up.
